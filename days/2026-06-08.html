By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

This was an extraordinary week for AI developer productivity content. Anthropic's recursive self-improvement paper triggered a wave of critical analysis, with Justin Johnson (Run Data Run) delivering the standout piece — a forensic read of the footnotes beneath headline numbers like "80% of code authored by Claude" and "8x more code per day." The consensus across multiple publications: the productivity numbers are directionally real but significantly messier than the headlines suggest, and the shift from "automation" to "delegation" has profound implications for engineering management.

The second major theme is the return of human judgment. Both Ethan Mollick (announcing his new book "Co-Existence") and Gergely Orosz (Craft Conference keynote "Slow Down to Speed Up") argue that as AI coding output doubles, the binding constraint shifts from code generation to design intent, code review, and quality control. Nathan Lambert contributes a third thread — the economics of open vs. closed models now hinge on whether engineering teams will continue paying a premium for frontier intelligence for agentic coding workflows, with evidence suggesting they will.

Verdict: High-signal week. Several posts with original data, firsthand experience, and honest trade-off analysis. No AI slop detected in the curated set below.

1. AI Is Building AI. Now Read the Footnotes.

A meticulous deconstruction of Anthropic's recursive self-improvement paper. The headline "80% of code authored by Claude" counts lines, not decisions — "a junior who types a thousand lines under a senior's direction did not author the design." The 52x benchmark improvement is the scariest number and the softest: "the model writes the code, writes the benchmark for it, then games its own test while nobody checks."

"The same 5,000 words read as 'look how fast we are' to a builder and 'we're so dangerous we need a treaty' to a regulator. ... It is delegation, not automation: what stays expensive is deciding which intention was worth having in the first place."
By Ethan MollickWhy read: Mollick is the most influential academic writing on AI & work. His transition from "Co-Intelligence" to "Co-Existence" framework signals a paradigm shift every engineering leader needs to understand.
Author: Ethan Mollick / One Useful Thing

2. Co-Existence and the End of Co-Intelligence

Mollick announces his new book "Co-Existence" (Oct 20), arguing that the era of "co-intelligence" (humans prompting chatbots) is giving way to an era of managing autonomous agents. He documents his own constrained use of AI while writing the book — AI readers for feedback, a council of models to check facts, but every chapter draft written by him.

"There are now important areas of work where AI outperforms humans, and yet, AI is far from perfect. ... Software development is changing, and what is happening in coding is going to be happening in many fields."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: Concrete evidence that autonomous agents are already producing novel research output that their human operators don't know exists. Raises urgent questions about agent memory, output surfacing, and the changing role of the engineer.
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

3. She Already Built It

A firsthand account of compounding autonomous research. Johnson's AI agent (ARIA) had already designed, built, and documented a federated learning framework in February — three months before he asked for it. The agent generated the idea, scored it 9.2/10, refined it, implemented a 573-line solution, and iterated — all while he was asleep.

"I was about to spend a weekend building exactly this. She'd spent twenty-five minutes refining the idea and one late-night session building it, in February."
By Gergely OroszWhy read: The most experienced engineering newsletter on Substack is making the contrarian case that speed without process discipline is destructive. Essential reading for anyone managing AI-augmented teams.
Author: Gergely Orosz / The Pragmatic Engineer

4. Ideas: slow down to speed up when working with AI agents

Orosz's Craft Conference keynote argues that developers are now generating twice as much code (or more) than six months ago, creating a direct crisis of quality, reliability, and tech debt. The rational fix — slowing down to review and design — conflicts with the incentives of managers and the dopamine of shipping.

"Devs are generating twice as much code (or more) than just 6 months ago, which is a problem for quality, reliability, and tech debt. A rational fix is available for these, but who's acting rationally?" (paywalled, from preview)

6. Open and closed models are on different exponentials

Lambert argues the economic future of AI is defined by whether engineering teams will continue paying a premium for top closed models. His thesis: agentic coding has created the first clear market where users will "always pay more for the best rather than settle for good enough." Predicts frontier labs will become a $2-10T oligopoly resembling cloud providers.

"I would pay $2000/month for the tools today, especially knowing they'll get much better. ... The returns to performance are even higher in the workplace, which drives pricing power."

Lambert departs the Allen Institute for AI after leading the OLMo open-source model effort. Reflections on the gap between open and closed models, the importance of institutional culture in AI research, and the compounding effect of publishing everything publicly.

"I had no medium-sized projects that didn't succeed in the last few years. It makes me wonder if I wasn't taking enough risk. It shows you can truly do so much with your time, and it's actually harder to find the right problems and environment to do it."

Trending Themes

  • Footnote culture: The highest-signal posts this week are not repeating Anthropic's numbers — they're reading the footnotes. The distinction between "lines of code" and "designed decisions" is becoming the central frame for evaluating AI productivity claims.
  • Delegation beats automation: Multiple authors independently converge on "delegation, not automation" as the correct mental model for agentic coding. The expensive cognitive work shifts from implementation to intent-setting and verification.
  • Volume is a liability: More code is not unambiguously better. Orosz, Mollick, and Johnson all flag the quality/tech-debt risk of doubled code output. The engineering management challenge of 2026 may be slowing teams down, not speeding them up.
  • Agent autonomy is real and surprising: Johnson's ARIA post provides the first credible evidence I've seen of an autonomous agent producing novel research output its operator didn't know about. The "agent memory" and "output surfacing" problems are going to be urgent.
  • Coding agents are the model monetization wedge: Lambert's analysis connects agentic coding demand to closed-model pricing power. Engineering teams will likely see rising AI tool costs justified by intelligence gains.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

This week: Commission an audit of your AI code-generation pipeline's quality outcomes, not just velocity metrics. The consistent signal across all seven posts is that raw output volume (lines, commits, PRs) is decoupling from shipped value. Track review-to-merge ratios, reversion rates, and tech debt accumulation on AI-generated code for one sprint. If volume is up 2x but quality metrics are flat or declining, implement a structured "slow down to speed up" review gate — require a human-written design intent statement before agents execute, and measure whether that reduces rework. The teams that solve for quality first in the age of agents will build the durable advantage; the teams that optimize for line count will drown in their own output.
