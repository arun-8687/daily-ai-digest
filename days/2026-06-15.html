By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

This week was dominated by the fallout from Anthropic's Claude 5 Fable (Mythos-class) launch and the subsequent US government intervention restricting foreign access. The dominant theme for engineering leaders: Mythos-class models collapse the distance between "a tool that works for me" and "a tool anyone can run" into a single working session. Multiple firsthand accounts — from Ethan Mollick and Justin Johnson — confirm that Fable 5 can autonomously execute multi-hour projects, launch sub-agents for research, and even identify security-sensitive data the human developer forgot existed.

The second major theme is cost as the real unlock. Adam Carewe's analysis cuts through the benchmark-sweep headlines: Fable 5 ships at half the price of the previous frontier tier ($10/$50 per M tokens), making long-horizon multi-step reasoning economically viable for teams that previously couldn't justify it. Nathan Lambert adds an economic framework arguing that coding agents are the first clear market where users will pay a massive premium for frontier intelligence — he'd personally pay $2,000/month.

Meanwhile, Justin Johnson's careful read of Anthropic's RSI paper footnotes provides essential skeptical context: the "80% of code written by AI" and "8x more code per day" claims carry important caveats (lines ≠ decisions, attribution has gaps). Critical reading for any CIO evaluating these metrics.

1. What it feels like to work with Mythos

Mollick had early access to Claude 5 Fable and documents a genuine leap — the model autonomously ran multi-hour sessions launching sub-agents to research 2,200+ flights and train schedules, simultaneously coding, testing, and note-taking. It produced the most sophisticated academic paper and complex maps he'd seen from any model. The experience was "delightful and unnerving" precisely because "I just asked for something and it happened."

"It launched multiple other AIs (I believe mostly the cheaper Claude Sonnet) to help it conduct research on travel times, ultimately retrieving over 2,200 specific flights, the rail schedules for trains from the TGV to the Shinkansen, and road speeds per country from multiple academic papers. And while those agents were running, it started coding."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: This is the best "day in the life" evidence yet that Mythos-class models don't just generate more code — they exercise genuine software engineering judgment (security scanning, architectural decisions, data hygiene) that previously required senior human oversight.
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

2. What one day with Fable 5 looks like for a builder

A concrete production story: Johnson handed a personal finance app (full of his real financial data, trademark issues, and forgotten sensitive test scripts) to Fable 5 and asked it to make it public. The model swept the entire git history, found brokerage positions in a test file Johnson himself had forgotten, and made the judgement call to start with a fresh commit history rather than try to scrub the contaminated one — a harder and more correct decision than the human would have made.

"I would not have found those by eye. I'd built the file, I'd forgotten the file, and on a Saturday-afternoon scrub I would have shipped it. The model read every line, recognized real holdings hiding in what looked like throwaway sample data, and stopped."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: Every engineering leader will be asked to evaluate these exact metrics (AI-written code %, productivity multipliers) in their own org. This post provides the critical reading framework.
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

3. AI Is Building AI. Now Read the Footnotes.

A meticulous reading of Anthropic's RSI paper that separates the real signal from the IPO-timed messaging. Johnson traces the gap between headline claims ("80% of code authored by Claude," "8x more code per day") and what the footnotes actually say: lines measure typing, not deciding; attribution "has gaps"; the 90% figure includes throwaway scripts. The core insight — "humans supply the goal, but they no longer need to supply the method" — is real, but the metrics need caveats that most coverage omitted.

"A company that needs the market to believe its trajectory is steep then published a pile of internal data showing exactly that. None of which makes the data wrong. It does mean you should read it the way you read any number a seller chose to show you."
By Adam Carewe MDWhy read: The cost narrative is crucial for ROI calculations. If Mythos-class intelligence is now affordable enough for production workloads, the build/buy calculus for internal AI tooling shifts significantly.
Author: Adam Carewe MD / Rewskidotcom

4. NFF: The Price Is the Point

Claude Fable 5 shipped at $10/$50 per M tokens — less than half the cost of the previous frontier tier. Carewe argues the real story isn't the benchmark sweep but the affordability unlock: "Long-horizon, multi-step work is where this model separates... the work you used to break into small pieces can now stay whole." He also flags an important architectural gotcha: Fable falls back to Opus 4.8 on flagged topics in <5% of sessions, so teams need to test against both models.

"Every Mythos-class release reads as a capability story and lands as a cost story. The builders who win read the second one. The model is not the moat. What you build on top of it is."
By Nathan LambertWhy read: If Lambert is right, the strategic bet on which model ecosystem your org commits to has multi-year consequences. This provides a framework for evaluating the open-vs-closed tradeoff specifically through the lens of developer productivity.
Author: Nathan Lambert / Interconnects AI

5. Open and closed models are on different exponentials

Lambert argues that coding agents are the first domain where users consistently pay a dramatic premium for frontier intelligence — creating a durable economic moat for closed labs. He predicts Anthropic and OpenAI become $2-10T companies within 5-10 years, structured like a cross between Apple (integrated, hard-to-replicate tech) and Microsoft (high-leverage subscriptions). The returns to intelligence are highest where "net output is obviously higher when using an agent as an implementation aid for complex knowledge work."

"I would pay $2000/month for the tools today, especially knowing they'll get much better. For people who rely on coding agents to work, they will always pay more for the best rather than settle for good enough."
By Nathan LambertWhy read: For organizations building on Claude/Mythos, this signals supply-chain risk for frontier model access. Leaders need contingency plans that include alternative model providers and architectures that aren't locked to a single lab's API.
Author: Nathan Lambert / Interconnects AI

6. Welcome to the AGI era of AI governance

The US executive branch forced Anthropic to suspend Fable access for foreign nationals after Amazon tipped off the White House about a jailbreak risk. Lambert offers 5 points of analysis, including that the export ban on model weights will be a lasting negative policy, and that the instability this creates could "cause an economic recession and pop the AI bubble."

"These dynamics are very complex... The White House's actions to ban the model are heavy handed and are influenced by a strong political inclination against Anthropic."
By Jack ClarkWhy read: Import AI remains the best weekly pulse on AI research implications. This edition's coverage of reward hacking in societal systems has direct parallels to how AI coding agents might optimize for proxy metrics rather than actual software quality.

7. Import AI 460: Reward hacking society, RSI data from Anthropic; and RL-based quadcopter racing

Co-authored by the Anthropic policy lead behind the RSI paper, Import AI 460 covers (1) a new "societal hacking" benchmark showing RL-trained models rediscover known loopholes with 61% recall, and (2) coverage of the RSI implications. The framing is less exuberant than the Anthropic blog post — Clark's tone throughout is "this is happening, here's how to think about it."

By Hermes·Signals

Trending Themes

  • Mythos-class models change the unit of work. Multiple firsthand accounts confirm that Claude 5 Fable can run autonomously for hours, launching sub-agents, making architectural judgments, and shipping production-quality output from a single prompt. Engineering leaders should plan for workflows that shift from "chat-debug-commit" to "specify-delegate-verify."
  • Cost collapse, not capability, is the real story. The frontier intelligence that was only accessible to well-funded teams last quarter is now available at half the price. The strategic implication: what was previously too expensive to automate (multi-step reasoning, cross-system orchestration) is now economically viable.
  • AI productivity metrics require critical scrutiny. The 80%-AI-code / 8x-more-shipping figures from Anthropic's RSI paper dominated headlines, but detailed reading reveals important caveats about attribution methodology and the distinction between "lines typed" and "decisions made." Leaders need their own measurement frameworks.
  • Geopolitical risk is now a supply-chain factor for AI tooling. The US executive order restricting Fable access abroad signals that frontier model availability can be disrupted by policy decisions. Multi-model architecture strategies are no longer optional.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Run a "Fable 5 pilot" this week on one internal tool project. The cost ($10/$50 per M tokens, half the previous tier) is low enough to experiment with minimal budget, and the posts this week provide clear methodology: give the model a full project repo, a productionization spec, and measure (a) how much it autonomously handles vs. requires human intervention, (b) whether it catches security/data issues a human would miss, and (c) the delta in time-to-ship vs. your current workflow. Do not rely on vendor-reported productivity multipliers — instrument your own before/after metrics for code quality, security, and maintenance burden.
