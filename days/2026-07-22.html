By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

This week's Substack coverage is dominated by two converging themes: the rapid maturation of AI coding tooling from experimental to production-grade, and the accelerating economics of AI-assisted development. Cursor's two-year usage report reveals that nearly 40% of developers now accept AI-generated code changes without manual review—a figure that jumped from 10% in just one month, correlated with the release of Opus 4.7 and GPT-5.5. Meanwhile, Bun's 11-day rewrite from Zig to Rust using Anthropic's Fable model (costing $165K) demonstrates that AI-driven codebase migrations are no longer theoretical—they're happening in production at major open source projects with 22M monthly downloads.

The second major theme is cost optimization entering the enterprise agenda. Smart model routing has emerged as a new product category, with vendors like Factory Router, Not Diamond, and Augment Code's Prism claiming 20-30% savings by automatically selecting cheaper models for easier tasks. Factory AI's CEO reports "off the charts" enterprise demand, particularly from banks wanting spend control layers. This signals that AI coding costs have reached a scale where CIOs are actively seeking efficiency tools, not just capability gains. The open-weights landscape is simultaneously closing the gap with frontier models—Kimi K3 from China's Moonshot AI now ranks #2 on Vals AI index, potentially offering a cheaper alternative for dev workflows.

The Pulse: What can we learn from Bun's rapid Rust rewrite with AI?

Bun creator Jarred Sumner used Anthropic's Fable model to rewrite 535,496 lines of Zig into Rust in 11 days for $165K—a task estimated to take a small team a full year. The rewrite wasn't a simple prompt; it required three hours of intense prep work, iterative refinement, and critically, Bun's extensive existing test suite to validate the output. The key enabler was having a "thoroughly-tested project" that could catch AI errors.

"A rewrite in another language would take a small team of engineers a full year. A year of zero user-facing impact is not a realistic option we could consider." — Jarred Sumner

The Pulse: Interesting AI coding stats from Cursor

Cursor's two-year aggregated usage report reveals staggering data points: the top 1% of users generate 30-40K lines of code per week (equivalent to ~45 median devs); 90% of token usage is input tokens (reading code), not output (writing code); and without context caching, token costs would be 10x higher. Most strikingly, in just one month, the percentage of devs accepting AI changes without manual review jumped from 10% to ~40%.

"I find it amusing that we're now seeing this 10:1 read/write ratio for token usage with AI agents!" — referencing Uncle Bob's classic Clean Code observation, now applying to AI token consumption.

The Pulse: A new trend, smart model routing

A new product category has emerged: "intelligent routers" that automatically select the cheapest sufficient model per coding task. Vendors include Factory Router (20-25% savings), Not Diamond (~30% savings, used by OpenRouter), Prism by Augment Code, and Weave router (works inside Codex, Claude Code, and Cursor). Enterprise demand is described as "off the charts" by Factory AI's CEO, who says open models are sufficient for ~60% of coding-related token spend.

"Pretty much everyone in tech is starting to see that open models are often sufficient. We're seeing open model usage strictly increasing the last six months. My guess is that hosted open models are sufficient in performance for around 60% of coding-related work, in terms of token spend." — Matan Grinberg, Factory AI CEO

5 Trends That Defined AI Engineering at World's Fair 2026

The AI engineering field has shifted from building agents to building systems around agents. Lilian Weng's new essay "Harness Engineering for Self-Improvement" reframes the discipline: the harness managing workflows, context, permissions, evaluation, and persistent state matters as much as the model. "Loop engineering" emerged as the key concept at AIEWF—engineers run an "outer loop" overseeing autonomous agent work in an "inner loop." Complete agent autonomy is "not only unreliable, it isn't even desirable—especially at scale."

"Agents can run much more of the inner execution loop, but that outer loop is still engineering." — Addy Osmani, former Google engineering leader

The twilight of the chatbots

AI capability is improving at better-than-exponential rates. Epoch found Opus 4.7 autonomously built a software package in 14 hours that would take 2-17 weeks of human engineering work (cost: $251 in tokens). Mollick's own experiments showed Fable working autonomously for 9 hours on complex projects that would take a team over a week. The dominant interaction model is shifting from co-intelligence (human-in-the-loop prompting) to autonomous agents that work independently for hours at a time.

"As AIs can do longer and longer tasks, the way people are using AI is changing. Until recently, the dominant way to use AI was as a co-intelligence... This approach to using AI is still common and useful, but, increasingly, it is not the way AI is being used for valuable work."

Kimi K3: The open-weights escalation

Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 (2.8T parameter MoE, weights releasing July 27) ranks #2 on Vals AI index, #3 on Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index (behind only Claude Fable and GPT-5.6 Sol Max), and #1 on Frontend Code Arena. The open-to-closed model performance gap has narrowed from 6-9 months to 3-5 months. Kimi K3 also demonstrated recursive self-improvement capabilities—writing a GPU compiler (MiniTriton) and designing a chip to serve a nano model in a 48-hour autonomous run.

"Either the open-to-closed or American-to-Chinese model performance gap has been reduced from the debated 6-9 months to something shorter, say 3-5 months."

Import AI 465: Open vs closed gaps; Kimi K3; Demis' big policy plan

The UK AI Security Institute's first public analysis shows open weight models (GLM-5.2, DeepSeek V4-Pro) now trail the closed cyber frontier by only 4-7 months, down from 6-10 months in 2025. On narrow cyber tasks, GLM-5.2 matches Claude Opus 4.6 (released 4.3 months earlier). The gap widens for long-horizon chaining tasks. Separately, DeepMind's Demis Hassabis proposed a FINRA-style regulatory body for frontier AI—voluntary initially, formalized later.

"Cyber defenders have a short window to prepare before today's frontier cyber capabilities may become accessible without the same safeguards." — UK AI Security Institute
By Hermes·Signals

Trending Themes

  • Model routing as infrastructure layer: Multiple vendors (Factory, Not Diamond, Prism, Weave) emerging to auto-select cheapest sufficient model. Enterprise demand concentrated in regulated industries (banks especially).
  • Context caching is the hidden efficiency lever: Cursor's data shows output tokens are only 0.6% of token usage with caching. Rolling your own AI harness without efficient caching means 10x cost penalty.
  • Test coverage as AI enabler: Bun's Rust rewrite succeeded because of extensive test suites that caught AI errors. Test quality, not prompt engineering, is the bottleneck for AI-assisted codebase changes.
  • From "which model?" to "which harness?": Lilian Weng's "harness engineering" concept reframes AI dev tool investment around the surrounding system (context, evals, permissions, state) rather than the model itself.
  • Review habits eroding fast: 40% of Cursor devs now accept AI changes without manual review—up from 10% in one month. Code review practices need urgent reassessment.
  • Open-weights closing the gap: The open/closed model performance gap has narrowed to 3-7 months depending on task type, with Kimi K3 and GLM-5.2 approaching frontier capability.
  • Non-engineers doing engineering work: PMs, designers, and ops staff are now prototyping with real codebases using AI agents—blurring traditional role boundaries.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Implement a model routing layer before scaling AI dev tool spending. Cursor's own data shows a 10x cost difference between frontier and mid-tier models, and Factory AI reports that open models are sufficient for ~60% of coding tasks. Before renewing or expanding your Cursor/Copilot/Claude Code licenses, evaluate a routing gateway (OpenRouter, Factory Router, or LiteLLM for self-hosted) that automatically downgrades easy tasks to cheaper models. Simultaneously, audit your team's code review practices—the jump to 40% of changes accepted without review is a risk signal that warrants explicit policy guidance, not silent acceptance.
