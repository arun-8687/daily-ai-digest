By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

Two big themes dominated Substack this week. First, the atomic unit of AI work has shifted from the model to the "skill" — Justin Johnson's Run Data Run and the upcoming ACM CAIS conference both signal that model quality is now table stakes; the real engineering frontier is the scaffolding, governance, and procedural knowledge that turns a frozen model into a dependable production agent. Second, a growing tension between AI-generated code volume and legacy workflows — Ali El-Shayeb documents exactly how teams that bolt AI onto 2015-era pipelines create compounding technical debt, while Ethan Mollick warns that the human cost of defaulting to AI (loss of skill development, cognitive overload from chatbot interfaces) is real and measurable. The Latent Space podcast with Cognition's Walden Yan reveals that Devin is already committing 80% of code unsupervised in production — we're past the "can it code?" question and into "how do we govern and validate what it produces?"

1. The Atomic Unit of Work Just Changed

The model has commoditized. The new atomic unit of agentic work is the "skill" — a named, versioned, governed piece of procedural knowledge that a frontier model executes with judgment. Johnson runs ~70 such skills across four machines. A skill is just a Markdown file in a folder — "a Word doc that happens to run" — that carries intent while the model supplies runtime judgment.

"A skill is a Word doc that happens to run. ... The unit carries the intent; the model supplies the judgment at runtime."
By Ethan MollickWhy read: The difference between AI that undermines capability and AI that enhances it is in interface design and workflow integration — a critical insight for CTOs designing how AI is deployed on their teams.
Author: Ethan Mollick / One Useful Thing

2. Choosing to Stay Human

Using AI as a default for writing and thinking carries hidden costs — it prevents the development of personal style and skill, and badly prompted AI writing produces "meaning-shaped attention vampires" that waste readers' cognitive effort. Mollick contrasts two education studies: one where ChatGPT access hurt learning (students got answers, not understanding), and another where an AI tutor tailored to individual needs boosted exam scores by the equivalent of 6-9 months of schooling.

"Balancing using AI with our own mental abilities is going to be a defining challenge of the coming years."
By Latent SpaceWhy read: 80% autonomous commit rate in production is a threshold that changes engineering management entirely. If you're still designing team structures around human-only commit authority, you're operating on assumptions that Cognition and OpenInspect have already invalidated.

3. The Age of Async Agents — Cognition's Walden Yan & OpenInspect's Cole Murray

Devin now commits 80% of code autonomously in production via spec-to-PR workflows. Walden Yan details how PMs are shipping code, how agent memory works across full VMs, and the shift from synchronous pairing to async agent-driven development. Key metrics: Cognition's agents operate with full VM environments, persistent memory, and a workflow where specs become PRs without human intervention.

"Spec-to-PR workflows, Full VMs, Agent Memory, and PMs Shipping Code" — the episode subtitle captures the paradigm shift.

4. The Model Is No Longer the Frontier

The first ACM Conference on AI and Agentic Systems (CAIS) program contains 61 peer-reviewed papers — almost none touch the model itself. The four research tracks are architectural patterns, system optimization, engineering operations, and evaluation. "Models stopped being the bottleneck. The thing that decides whether an agent works in production is the scaffolding." Johnson's key frame: the boring papers (agent specification formats, model gateways, self-evolving memory) are the roadmap; the exciting ones are scenery.

"the boring papers are the roadmap, and the exciting ones are the scenery"

5. Your Team Adopted AI Tools. Your Workflows Didn't.

41% of worldwide code is now AI-generated, but most teams still run 2015-era code review, testing, and deployment workflows. A fintech with 120 engineers saw velocity go up 3x but QA didn't change — within 3 months they had a 200-feature backlog and bug escape rate doubled. The fix: rebuild testing around autonomous validation that validates AI-generated code in real-time, reducing feature-to-production time from 6 hours to under 2 hours.

"When AI 10x'd the code output, every downstream process became a bottleneck. ... AI coding tools aren't faster typewriters. They're a complete paradigm shift."
By Gergely OroszWhy read: If the top AI labs are hiring FDEs aggressively, it confirms that model capability alone isn't enough — deployment and integration talent is the binding constraint.
Author: Gergely Orosz / The Pragmatic Engineer

6. The Pulse: Forward Deployed Engineering Heats Up Again

Demand for Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) roles is surging at Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic. The role has evolved to resemble a consultant/solution architect hybrid, often staffed by early-junior engineers. This signals that AI companies need field engineers who can bridge the gap between powerful models and messy real-world integrations.

"Massive demand for the role at Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic. The latest version of the FDE role looks like the consultant / solution architect role done by many early-junior engineers."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: A practical blueprint for heterogeneous multi-agent systems. Most teams are trying to build one generalist agent; the better strategy is a squad of cheap, specialized agents with strong coordination.

7. Eight Agents, One Fight

Johnson runs 8 autonomous agents (5 LLMs, 3 runtimes, 4 hosts) that share an Obsidian vault. Two agents reading the same news story wrote completely different reports — one through an AI-in-production lens, the other through a clinical-validation lens. The key insight: running two specialists instead of one generalist costs almost nothing operationally, but the cost of a single-worldview write-up is high and silent.

"The operational cost of running two specialists instead of one generalist is small. The cost of a single-worldview write-up on a story that needed two is high and silent. You don't notice the angle you didn't get."

By Hermes·Signals

Trending Themes

  • Scaffolding over models. The consensus across Johnson, CAIS conference, and the Latent Space episodes is clear: model quality is commoditizing, and the real engineering challenge is the system around the model — skill libraries, agent memory, evaluation, governance.
  • Workflow mismatch crisis. Multiple authors (El-Shayeb, Mollick, Orosz) independently identified the same pattern: AI generates code/output faster than legacy validation workflows can handle. The bottleneck has moved from writing to validation.
  • Multi-agent heterogeneity wins. Johnson's agent squad architecture and Cognition's spec-to-PR pipeline both point away from monolithic agents and toward specialized, coordinated agent teams.
  • The human cost is real. Mollick's piece on the cognitive tax of chatbot interfaces and Orosz's post on "the grief when AI writes most of the code" both address the psychological and skill-development costs of AI in engineering that are rarely discussed in productivity metrics.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Audit your validation pipeline this quarter. If your team has adopted AI coding tools (Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code) but still uses human-paced code review, manual testing sprints, and gated deployment cycles, you are accruing technical debt faster than you realize. Run the experiment: measure time from "AI generates code" to "production deploy" and compare it to the pre-AI baseline. If validation time hasn't collapsed alongside writing time, you have a workflow mismatch. The fix — autonomous validation at every layer, continuous deployment with automated rollback, and a skill library for procedural knowledge — is the single highest-ROI engineering investment for the next 18 months.
