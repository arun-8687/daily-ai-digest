The Day in Review

This week's Substack landscape is dominated by a single, converging theme: **AI engineering has shifted from building with agents to building systems around agents**. The Pragmatic Engineer's rare inside look at Anthropic reveals that even at the most AI-pilled company on earth, verification now takes longer than implementation, code review is increasingly done by AI, and the Bun-to-Rust rewrite (535K lines, 11 days, $165K in tokens) is the new benchmark for what's possible. Meanwhile, Latent.Space's coverage of the AI Engineer World's Fair identifies "loop engineering" and "harness engineering" as the discipline's new control layer—human engineers occupying the outer loop while agents run the inner loop autonomously.

The second major theme is workforce bifurcation and disillusionment. Lenny Rachitsky's annual tech worker sentiment survey (598 likes, 92 restacks) shows the workforce splitting into two camps: those "amplified by AI" and those "shaken by it," with burnout jumping 11 points in a year. AI Supremacy's "Great AI Disappointment" thesis takes this further, arguing generative AI is failing to deliver on societal promises. Ethan Mollick's practical guide cuts through both the hype and the doom, offering a clear, opinionated framework for which AI to actually use—and it's no longer just chatbots, it's agentic systems.

How Building Software is Changing at Anthropic

An on-the-ground report from inside Anthropic reveals that verification now consumes 85% of engineering time vs 15% for implementation, code review and security scanning are increasingly AI-run, and the Bun runtime was rewritten from Zig to Rust (535K lines) in 11 days using 64 parallel Claude agents and $165K in tokens. Complex infra projects (Claude Managed Agents) still took 6 months and required PRDs, re-architecting, and cross-team alignment—proving that AI doesn't eliminate the need for planning on hard problems. Anthropic deleted 80% of Claude Code's system prompt because newer models are smarter. The more hands-on engineers get with AI, the less they fear job displacement.

"We see that few tokens are spent on actual implementation. Most are spent on discovery of unknowns, prototyping, mocking, and then in verification and testing." — Thariq Shihipar, Anthropic

5 Trends That Defined AI Engineering at World's Fair 2026

From the AI Engineer World's Fair, five trends emerged: (1) focus shifts from agents to the systems around them (harness engineering); (2) "loop engineering" becomes the new control layer with humans in the outer loop; (3) AI engineering enters the enterprise via "forward deployed engineers" (FDEs); (4) coding agents replace IDEs as the developer interface; (5) every platform builds around "agent skills" (declarative Markdown files encoding workflows). Lilian Weng's pivot from her 2023 "LLM Powered Autonomous Agents" to her 2026 "Harness Engineering for Self-Improvement" essay captures the maturation.

"Complete agent autonomy is not only unreliable, it isn't even desirable — especially at scale. So it was a relief that at AIEWF, agents were largely positioned as augmenting the AI engineer, rather than replacing them."

Import AI 466: The Bitter Lesson for Robotics, AIs Complete Week-Long Programming Tasks

Epoch and METR released MirrorCode, a benchmark testing whether AI can re-implement software programs from CLI access only (no source code, no web). Claude Opus 4.7 reimplemented Apple's pkl (61K lines) in 14 hours for $251—a task estimated to take a human 2-17 weeks. 17/25 target programs had at least one perfect-scoring run. Separately, Anthropic demonstrated that Opus 4.7 acting autonomously completed robot tasks in 9 minutes that took humans 181 minutes a year prior—a 20x improvement from scaling general-purpose models alone, not robotics-specific training.

"AI systems can self-orient with regard to their environment... purely through input-output access to it they're able to write from the ground up their own implementation of it. This suggests that very smart AI agents may be able to learn from the world in such a way that they can recapitulate things they interface with as homegrown capabilities."

How Tech Workers Are Feeling in 2026: A Workforce Splitting in Two

Their second annual survey reveals a bifurcated workforce: one half feels "amplified by AI," the other "shaken by it"—and this divide predicts career sentiment more than role, seniority, or company size. Burnout jumped from 44.7% to 55.7% in one year. 82% say AI makes them more productive, but 53% wouldn't recommend their field to newcomers. Only 22% fear losing their job to AI; far more (51%) fear being expected to do more for the same pay. The defining emotion about AI is ambivalence (77% selected both positive and negative emotions).

"One half feels amplified by AI—more capable, more confident, more excited than they've been in their entire career. The other half feels shaken by it—less sure of their value and whether there's still a place for them."

Ontologies Are So Back: Why AI Agents Are Reviving the Semantic Web

AI engineers are rediscovering ontologies—graph-based knowledge representations—as deterministic guardrails for probabilistic agents. UC Berkeley's Frank Coyle and Neo4j CEO Emil Eifrem outlined three ontology types for agent systems: business-facing, technical (metadata), and execution traces. The key insight is that web ontologies (Schema.org, FOAF, Dublin Core) are already in LLM training data, so developers can prompt for them rather than reinventing from scratch. This convergence of symbolic and neural approaches is being called "neurosymbolic AI."

What is "Loop Engineering"?

Orosz investigates the buzzword "loop engineering" and finds it's a real but evolving concept: triggers and cron jobs that kick off AI agent loops to autonomously handle tasks like code review, issue triage, and testing. He notes much of what's being called "loop engineering" is essentially sophisticated automation with AI agents replacing what used to require human-initiated workflows. The post includes honest assessment that some of it is "AI slop"—overblown hype—and questions whether the term will stick or is "here today, gone tomorrow."

Trending Themes

  • Verification > Implementation: Across Anthropic, the AI Engineer World's Fair, and the Bun rewrite, the consensus is that validating AI output now takes significantly more time than generating it. The new engineering ratio is ~15% implementation, ~85% verification.
  • Harness engineering as discipline: Lilian Weng's evolution from "LLM Powered Autonomous Agents" (2023) to "Harness Engineering for Self-Improvement" (2026) crystallizes the field's maturation—the system around the model matters as much as the model itself.
  • Loop engineering & outer-loop control: Humans occupying the "outer loop" (oversight, direction-setting) while agents run the "inner loop" (execution) is becoming the standard architecture, discussed at both Pragmatic Engineer and Latent.Space.
  • Agent skills as declarative Markdown: The shift from orchestration code (Python) to declarative Markdown files encoding workflows and best practices, popularized by Anthropic's agent skills and Google DeepMind's "agents are just files" approach.
  • Workforce bifurcation: Multiple publications converge on the same finding—AI is splitting workers into "amplified" vs. "shaken" camps, with burnout rising and field recommendation rates falling despite productivity gains.
  • Cost transparency: Concrete token costs are appearing in case studies ($165K for Bun rewrite, $251 for MirrorCode task, $100-400 for gotree reimplementation), enabling real ROI modeling.
  • AI coding benchmarks getting "too easy": MirrorCode creators note leading models went from ~30% to solving 17/25 programs in a year, raising questions about benchmark longevity.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Audit your engineering organization's verification infrastructure. The data from Anthropic, the AI Engineer World's Fair, and the Bun rewrite all point to the same shift: implementation is now cheap and fast, but verification (code review, security scanning, fuzz testing, out-of-process testing) is the new bottleneck—and the new competitive advantage. Organizations that invest in automated verification pipelines (AI code review, security scanners, test generation) will be able to safely run 64 parallel agents and ship rewrites in 11 days. Those that don't will either move too slowly or ship unverified code at dangerous velocity. Start by ensuring every AI-assisted PR passes through: (1) automated AI code review, (2) security scanning, (3) out-of-process test execution, and (4) a human gate for high-blast-radius changes. The Anthropic model of "another Claude with a fresh context window judges if it's simple and low blast-radius → auto-merge" is the endpoint to aim toward.
