By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

The dominant theme this week is the transition from co-intelligence to autonomous AI agents — and what it means for engineering leaders, hiring, and organizational strategy. Ethan Mollick's "The twilight of the chatbots" crystallizes the shift: AI systems can now work autonomously for 9-14 hours on complex software projects that would take human teams weeks, and capability gains are accelerating on a better-than-exponential curve. Meanwhile, Justin Johnson's analysis of Anthropic's internal 400,000-session Claude Code study provides the most important data point of the month: domain expertise — not coding ability — is the primary predictor of success with AI coding tools. Marketers and staff engineers finish coding tasks at nearly the same rate, but domain experts succeed at roughly double the rate of novices.

A second powerful thread runs through multiple posts: the model is the commodity, the harness is the moat. Justin Johnson's "The Harness Is the Moat" and Nathan Lambert's "Open and closed models are on different exponentials" both argue that durable advantage now lives in the system built around the model — the tools, conventions, memory, and feedback loops — not in which frontier model you license. Lambert makes the economic case that coding agents are creating the first market where buyers will pay a substantial premium for the best intelligence, predicting a $2-10T oligopoly for frontier labs.

1. The twilight of the chatbots

AI capability gains are accelerating on a better-than-exponential curve. METR, the UK AI Security Institute, and Epoch all show that AI can now complete work equivalent to 2-17 weeks of human engineering effort in a single 14-hour autonomous session ($251 in tokens). The dominant use paradigm is shifting from "co-intelligence" (human-in-the-loop prompting) to long-running, self-correcting autonomous agents. Open-weights Chinese models lag 6-12 months behind but are on their own exponential trajectory.

"As AIs can do longer and longer tasks, the way people are using AI is changing... Long-running, smart, and self-correcting AI systems do not need constant human intervention, and they require a different way of working."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: The premium on being a programmer collapsed; the premium on understanding the problem held. This fundamentally changes how to staff, train, and evaluate engineering teams.
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

2. You Don't Have to Write the Code

Anthropic analyzed ~400,000 real Claude Code sessions and found that job title barely predicts success with coding agents: software occupations succeeded at nearly the same rate as non-software occupations. What actually matters is domain expertise — novices succeeded ~42% of the time while domain experts landed between ~75-88%. In a typical session, the human makes ~5 decisions and the agent makes ~45. The labor didn't disappear — it separated. People decide what to build, agents decide how to build it.

"What predicted success was not your job title, and it was not whether you could write code. It was whether you understood the problem you were trying to solve."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: Leaders who focus their teams on selecting "the best model" are optimizing the wrong variable. The real investment should be in the scaffolding, evaluation infrastructure, and domain-specific tooling that compound over time.
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

3. The Harness Is the Moat

The model is the commodity layer — you can rent it, switch it, and so can your competitor. The durable advantage is the "harness": everything you build around the model (memory, conventions, tools, feedback loops, quality gates, context). Johnson open-sourced his own harness (Claudelicious) to show the shape of it. The first academic conference on agentic systems had almost no papers touching the model itself — the frontier moved to the system around the model.

"A model is a thing you rent for an hour. A harness is a thing you live inside."
By Ethan MollickWhy read: Demonstrates that frontier models have crossed a threshold where they can orchestrate sub-agents, conduct research, and execute complex multi-hour tasks autonomously. The experience is "somewhere between delightful and unnerving."
Author: Ethan Mollick / One Useful Thing

4. What it feels like to work with Mythos

Firsthand account of Claude 5 Fable (a Mythos-class model). It worked autonomously for multiple hours on multi-page specifications, launching sub-agents (Claude Sonnet) to conduct research, retrieving 2,200+ specific flights and rail schedules, then coding, testing, and verifying in parallel. Built an isochrone map from a single prompt that no previous model could even approximate. Created games, academic papers, and 10-page poems entirely from math-based rendering.

"It launched multiple other AIs to help it conduct research... And while those agents were running, it started coding. Then it launched yet more agents and tests to verify its code, all the while taking notes about its progress."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: Leaders need to read vendor data critically. The underlying trend (AI accelerating its own development) is real, but the claims are exaggerated for fundraising. The real takeaway: "humans supply the goal, but they no longer need to supply the method."
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

5. AI Is Building AI. Now Read the Footnotes.

Critical analysis of Anthropic's recursive self-improvement paper. The headline numbers (80%+ of merged code "authored by Claude," engineers shipping 8x more code per day, Claude improving an internal benchmark 52x vs. 4x for humans) are real but come with footnotes nearly every headline omitted. Most of that 80% is Claude typing while a human points and edits. The paper was released ahead of a ~$65B funding round and potential IPO, making it a flex and a warning simultaneously.

"The line that carries the whole argument is this: 'humans supply the goal, but they no longer need to supply the method.'"
By Nathan LambertWhy read: For procurement decisions: invest premium budget in frontier models for coding agent workloads where intelligence directly compounds output. Consider open models for lower-stakes, cost-sensitive applications.
Author: Nathan Lambert / Interconnects AI

6. Open and closed models are on different exponentials

Coding agents have created the first clear market where users will pay a substantial premium for marginal intelligence gains. Beyond the Opus 4.5 and Codex 5.2 thresholds, users' net output is obviously higher with the best models. Lambert predicts frontier labs will become an oligopoly valued at $2-10T, combining Apple-like integrated technology with Microsoft-like subscription economics. Open models lag on a different exponential curve — good enough for many use cases, but not where intelligence directly drives revenue.

"I would pay $2000/month for the tools today, especially knowing they'll get much better."
By Joe HudsonWhy read: As AI commoditizes technical execution, the human differentiators are emotional regulation, conflict navigation, and judgment under uncertainty — skills that can be trained but are currently undervalued in engineering organizations.
Author: Joe Hudson / Lenny's Newsletter

7. The new inner game: Your unfair advantage in the age of AI

An executive coach who works with OpenAI's research team argues that the traditional skills of effort and knowledge are becoming exactly what AI does best. The scarce skill in an AI-abundant world is emotional clarity: the ability to feel what you're feeling without being run by it. Teams that thrive in "AI-pilled" environments can stay in difficult conversations, not turn on themselves under pressure, and keep going through failure.

"When knowledge and effort are nearly free, emotional clarity is scarce. But luckily, it's a skill that can be learned."
By Ali El-ShayebWhy read: The "assistant model" of AI adoption (giving people AI tools and asking them to work faster) is a dead end. Real scaling requires architectural changes — specialized agents, RAG pipelines, and human-in-the-loop validation.
Author: Ali El-Shayeb / Islands

8. How to scale content creation without adding headcount

A practical production framework for moving from "AI assistant" (human still in the bottleneck) to autonomous content supply chains. Key results: 3x content volume without headcount increase, 40% lower cost per asset, 2-day turnaround from topic to published post, SEO traffic growth per asset improved. The bottleneck isn't writing — it's research and structuring. Internal data ingestion (Slack, Fathom, Notion) is the primary fuel.

"The real shift happens when you move from assistants to autonomous supply chains. This means building systems where AI agents handle the heavy lifting of generation without constant human intervention."

Trending Themes

  • The collapse of the programming premium: Multiple data sources (Anthropic's 400K-session study, Addy Osmani's practitioner observations, Ethan Mollick's experiments) converge on the same finding — coding ability is no longer the bottleneck. Domain expertise and problem understanding are the new scarce resources.
  • Harness over model: Three separate publications (Johnson, Lambert, Mollick) independently argue that the durable competitive advantage comes from the system built around the model, not the model itself. The "model selection" debate is consuming energy that should go into tooling, evaluation, and workflow design.
  • Autonomous agents cross the threshold: Mythos-class models (Claude Fable, GPT-5.6) can now work autonomously for 9-14 hours, launch sub-agents, conduct research, and execute complex multi-spec projects. The interface paradigm is shifting from chat to delegation.
  • Open vs. closed divergence: Frontier closed models and open-weights models are on different exponential curves. For coding agent workloads where intelligence compounds output, the premium for closed models is justified. For many other use cases, open models are sufficient and rapidly improving.
  • RSI is happening — with caveats: Anthropic's data shows AI accelerating its own development (80%+ of code AI-authored, 8x throughput), but the claims are interwoven with IPO positioning. The trend is real; the magnitude is worth scrutinizing.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Redesign your engineering operating model around delegation, not assistance. The evidence this week is overwhelming: the "AI chatbot as copilot" paradigm is already outdated. Within 90 days, pilot a structured engineering workflow where your most senior domain experts spend 70% of their time on problem definition and verification strategy, and 30% on execution — reversing the traditional ratio. Measure the delta in output quality between domain experts and generalists using the same AI tools. The data from Anthropic's 400K-session study suggests this single organizational change could double your engineering team's effective throughput. Simultaneously, invest in building your "harness" — the institutional memory, conventions, evaluation infrastructure, and tooling layer that compounds over time — rather than cycling through model selection debates. The frontier model you choose today will be obsolete in weeks; the harness you build will be the thing that makes your team uniquely effective with every future model.
