By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

The dominant theme this week is a paradigm shift from chatbots to autonomous agents, and the ripple effects across engineering teams and strategy. Ethan Mollick's latest post argues we're in "the twilight of the chatbots" — AI capability gains are accelerating faster than exponential, and the dominant usage pattern is shifting from co-intelligence (prompting step-by-step) to delegation (setting agents loose on multi-hour tasks). Justin Johnson's analysis of Anthropic's 400,000 Claude Code sessions provides hard data backing this: success with AI coding tools isn't about programming skill — it's about domain expertise. Meanwhile, the US government's restriction of Mythos-class models has triggered a realignment toward open-weight models (GLM-5.2 emerging as a surprise leader) and rising skepticism about the ROI of expensive closed-source AI agents, a shift Michael Spencer calls "The Token Apocalypse."

The workforce itself is bifurcating: Lenny's 2026 tech worker survey shows 82% reporting higher productivity from AI, yet burnout jumped 11 points year-over-year, and 53% would not recommend their field to newcomers. The signal for engineering leaders is clear: invest in domain expertise and the "harness" around models, not the models themselves.

1. The twilight of the chatbots

AI capability gains (measured by METR, UK AISI, and Epoch) are increasing at a better-than-exponential rate. Epoch found Opus 4.7 working autonomously for 14 hours built a software package worth 2–17 weeks of human engineering work for $251 in tokens. The dominant usage pattern is shifting from "co-intelligence" (step-by-step human guidance) to delegation: long-running, self-correcting AI systems that don't need constant human intervention. Open-weight Chinese models are climbing their own exponential curve, lagging US frontier models by 6–12 months.

"Long-running, smart, and self-correcting AI systems do not need constant human intervention, and they require a different way of working... The twilight of the chatbots gives way to the dawn of the agents."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: The ROI of AI coding tools depends more on your team's domain expertise than their coding skills. Hire and train for problem understanding, not syntax fluency.
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

2. You Don't Have to Write the Code

Anthropic analyzed ~400,000 real Claude Code sessions and found that what predicts success is not job title or coding ability — it's domain expertise. Software occupations succeeded at nearly the same rate as non-programmers on coding tasks. What collapsed was the premium on being a programmer; what held was the premium on understanding the problem. Novice sessions succeeded ~30% of the time vs. ~60% for domain experts. In a typical session, the human made ~1 decision per minute; the agent made ~7 — people decided what to build, the agent decided how to build it.

"The thing you spent fifteen years getting good at just became the thing that decides whether the most expensive tool you're buying actually pays off."
By Ethan MollickWhy read: Demonstrates what "agent-native" development looks like: models orchestrating other models, running parallel research tasks, verifying their own output. The engineering management challenge shifts from writing specs to reading agent logs.
Author: Ethan Mollick / One Useful Thing

3. What it feels like to work with Mythos

First hands-on account of Claude Fable 5 (Mythos-class). Worked autonomously up to 12 hours on multi-page specs. In one striking example, it was asked to build an isochrone map — it launched multiple sub-agents to research 2,200+ flights, TGV/Shinkansen schedules, and road speeds from academic papers simultaneously while coding, then launched more agents to verify its own code. Created sophisticated games and a 10-page rhyming poem from single prompts.

"[It was] somewhere between delightful and unnerving. Delightful because I just asked for something and it happened. And also unnerving because I just asked for something and it happened."
By Noam Segal & Lenny RachitskyWhy read: Productivity gains from AI are real but unevenly distributed. Leaders must manage the psychological divide: tools that amplify senior engineers may destabilize juniors. Burnout risk rises when AI accelerates output without changing expectations.

4. How tech workers are feeling in 2026: a workforce splitting in two

Second annual tech worker survey (large sample). 82% say AI makes them measurably more productive, but burnout jumped from 44.7% to 55.7% year-over-year. Career optimism fell from 54.8% to 48.7%. The workforce is bifurcating: one half feels "amplified by AI — more capable, more confident, more excited than they've been in their entire career"; the other half feels "shaken by it — less sure of their value." 53% would steer a newcomer away from their own field.

"One half feels amplified by AI... The other half feels shaken by it. Which side of that line people fall on predicts how they feel about their career more than anything else, including their current role, seniority, or company size."

Author: Michael Spencer / AI Supremacy

5. The Token Apocalypse

The US government's June 12 restriction of Mythos-class models has seriously damaged trust in closed-source AI. Palantir/DoD openly questioning closed models' value proposition. "Token-maxxing" (high Claude Code bills) and AI agents failing to demonstrate value at scale have led to a pivot toward open-weight Chinese models. Spencer argues "vibe coding failed to contribute meaningful products at scale" in 2025, and 2026 is seeing "token minimization" as enterprises tighten AI spending.

"If 2025 was the year where vibe coding failed to contribute meaningful products at scale, 2026 is the year when tokemaxxing led to high Claude code bills and became token-minimization where AI agents failed to demonstrate as much value as it was hoped."
By Nathan LambertWhy read: The open-weight ecosystem now has a credible alternative to closed frontier models for agentic coding workflows. For cost-conscious engineering orgs, this changes the build-vs-buy calculus for AI coding infrastructure.
Author: Nathan Lambert / Interconnects AI

6. GLM-5.2 is the step change for open agents

GLM-5.2 from China's Z.ai represents a "DeepSeek R1-level moment" for open models — the first open model that genuinely competes with closed frontier models on agent benchmarks. It's the only open model mixing it up with OpenAI and Anthropic on Arena's agent leaderboard, even beating Claude Fable on Design Arena. The release triggered community excitement comparable to DeepSeek R1 and Kimi K2. MIT-licensed weights.

"A small change in benchmarks and training can open a wide range of new use-cases... GLM-5.2 has consolidated the top of the reputational market."

7. Not all AI agents are created equal

A practical framework for categorizing agent initiatives into three architectural tiers. Teams often try to prioritize fundamentally different kinds of systems (a RAG copilot vs. a voice shopping agent vs. a code review agent) as if they're the same thing. One agent takes six weeks to build, another takes six months; one costs $500/month to operate, another could generate a six-figure annual LLM bill. The authors provide a hierarchy-based approach to agent prioritization.

"Treating architecturally different products as if they're in the same category makes effective prioritization nearly impossible. Prioritization breaks not because teams are bad at planning but because they're comparing apples, oranges, and jet engines on the same spreadsheet."

A roundup of practical tooling updates: Claude Tag now lets you @mention a shared Claude Code instance in Slack for team-wide agent collaboration; Gemini 3.5 Flash gained computer use (browser/mobile/desktop control); Notion's new dev platform allows external agents (Claude Code, Cursor, Codex) to work from shared docs and task boards; OpenAI built its first AI chip (Jalapeño, with Broadcom) for inference workloads behind Codex and agent products.

Trending Themes

  • From chatbots to agents: The dominant paradigm is shifting — Ethan Mollick calls it "the twilight of the chatbots." Engineering leaders should plan for a world where agents run autonomously for hours, not where humans prompt step-by-step.
  • Domain expertise > coding skill: The Anthropic/Claude Code telemetry (400k sessions) conclusively shows that problem understanding predicts success more than programming ability. This changes hiring, training, and team structure.
  • The workforce bifurcation: AI creates a productivity divide within teams. Those who can leverage it feel amplified; those who can't feel threatened. Burnout is rising even as productivity gains materialize.
  • Open-weight alternatives are real: GLM-5.2's agent performance, combined with US government restrictions on closed models, makes the open-weight ecosystem a credible alternative for production workloads.
  • Token economics are under pressure: "Token apocalypse" discourse reflects real concerns about the cost of agentic AI at scale. The unit economics of autonomy (hours of agent runtime) are now a boardroom topic.
  • Geopolitical risk is now technical risk: US restrictions on Mythos demonstrated that closed-model dependency carries real supply-chain risk. Model diversity is becoming a risk management requirement.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Run a "domain expertise audit" on your AI-enabled teams this quarter. The Anthropic data shows that 400,000 sessions of Claude Code telemetry correlates success with problem understanding, not programming skill. Identify the domains where your team has the deepest expertise and prioritize agentic AI deployment there first. Pair domain experts (even non-programmers) with AI coding tools — they'll get roughly 2x the success rate of novices. Simultaneously, commission a model-diversity review: what percentage of your AI spend is on models subject to geopolitical restriction? GLM-5.2's agent performance at MIT-licensed cost suggests it's time to build an open-weight fallback into your AI infrastructure strategy.
