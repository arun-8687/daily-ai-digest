By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

This week's most important theme across Substack is the maturation of "loop engineering" from a hand-rolled hack into a recognized discipline — and the honest reassessment that follows. The Pragmatic Engineer, Latent Space, and Run Data Run all converge on the same insight from different angles: the loop itself is the easy part; the hard part is everything around it — checks, costs, trust, and the org's ability to absorb the output. Boris Cherny (Anthropic/Claude Code) says he no longer prompts, he writes loops; Geoffrey Huntley's "Ralph" loop is now a year old and shipping as /loop and /goal commands in major harnesses. But practitioners report agents drifting, token costs spiraling (Uber allegedly burned its annual AI coding budget in four months), and a 24% PR-merge lift at Microsoft being the sharpest measured number vs. the 10x claims circulating on social media.

A second, equally important theme is the bifurcation of the tech workforce. Lenny Rachitsky and Noam Segal's annual sentiment survey (n large enough to be credible) finds tech workers splitting into two populations — those "amplified by AI" and those "shaken by it" — with that single variable predicting career outlook more than seniority, role, or company size. Burnout rose 11 points in a year. Only 22% fear job loss to AI; far more (51%) fear being expected to do more for the same pay. Ethan Mollick independently argues we are "inside an exponential," moving from co-intelligence (chatbots) to delegation (agents), and that the managerial skill of assigning work to agents is becoming the new core competency.

Third: open-weights models are at a policy inflection point. Nathan Lambert reports serious White House discussions about banning or indefinitely delaying open-weight models above the capability of GPT-5.5 / Opus 4.8 / GLM-5.2 within the next 6 months. GLM-5.2 itself is being called the "step change for open agents" — the first open model mixing it up with frontier closed models on agent benchmarks. For engineering leaders, this means the cheap, self-hostable coding model option may not be durable; strategy should not assume it.

What is "loop engineering?"

Loop engineering — designing the system that prompts the agent rather than prompting it yourself — has gone from Geoffrey Huntley's "Ralph" Bash loop a year ago to a shipped feature (/loop, /goal) in all major coding harnesses by May 2026. But practitioner feedback is mixed: agents drift, "human in the loop" often outperforms, and at API-priced tokens the practice "gets expensive fast." Max Kanat-Alexander argues the loop may have been a temporary hack while harnesses caught up; most devs may benefit more from context engineering than loop engineering.

"I don't prompt Claude anymore. I have loops running that prompt Claude and figuring out what to do. My job is to write loops." — Boris Cherny, creator of Claude Code, at Anthropic's developer conference.

It Spreads Sideways. Someone Still Has to Light It.

Compares three productivity numbers circulating the same week: Cherny's "10x" anecdotes, Replit's "3x in six months," and a Microsoft telemetry study of tens of thousands of engineers showing a measured 24% lift in merged PRs over four months. The pattern: "the number falls as the instrument sharpens." Microsoft's social-network analysis is the actionable finding — engineers whose skip-level peers used the tools had 216% higher odds of adopting; engineers whose direct manager used them had 82% higher odds of trying. But managers themselves were no more likely to adopt than any individual contributor. The bottleneck column in Cherny's own five-rung ladder contains zero model constraints — every limit is attention, review, and trust.

"An engineer whose manager used Copilot CLI had higher odds of both trying it (+82%) and, more modestly, sticking with it (+22%). … The one person whose adoption moves everybody else's is no more likely than anybody else to adopt."

The Loop Is Simpler Than It Sounds

A loop is ~6 lines of code: hand the agent a spec, let it do one task, save state to disk, wipe its context, restart fresh. The reason it works is counterintuitive — the fresh worker "never drowns in its own earlier confusion." But three questions decide whether a loop pays off or burns you: (1) How do you know it worked? — the part that says "no" must be built first; at 85% per-step success a 10-step job completes only ~20% of the time. (2) What did it cost? — same task, same model ran $8 to $240; one team spent $40K just on verification runs. (3) The model's memory is scratch paper; the real state lives on disk.

"Even if every step in a job succeeds 85% of the time, a ten-step job finishes correctly only about 20% of the time. … The agents 'diagnose a failure in perfect detail and still do nothing to recover from it.'"

How tech workers are feeling in 2026: a workforce splitting in two

Second annual tech-worker sentiment survey. The single strongest predictor of career outlook is whether a worker feels "amplified by AI" or "shaken by it" — more than title, tenure, or company. Burnout rose from 44.7% to 55.7% in one year; career optimism fell from 54.8% to 48.7%. 82% say AI makes them measurably more productive, but many worry the gains come at the cost of "sharpness." Only 22% fear losing their job to AI; 51% fear being expected to do more for the same pay. 53% would steer newcomers away from their own field.

"The workforce is bifurcating into two realities. … Everyone agrees the ground is moving. No one is sure yet if it's an earthquake or a launch."

5 Trends That Defined AI Engineering at World's Fair 2026

Five trends from AIEWF 2026: (1) Focus shifted from agents to the systems around them — Lilian Weng's 2026 "Harness Engineering" essay supersedes her 2023 "LLM Powered Autonomous Agents." (2) Loop engineering is the new control layer; "the agent runs the inner execution loop; I set the direction and make decisions in the outer loop" (Steinberger). (3) AI engineering enters the enterprise via "Forward Deployed Engineers" — Cursor's FDEs guarantee "strict ROI" before they walk away. (4) Coding agents are replacing IDEs as the developer interface. (5) Every agent platform is building around "skills" — declarative Markdown files that reduce orchestration code. Geoffrey Huntley's caution: "My biggest concern is that this time next year … we're going to see a whole bunch of folks saying, our factories failed, our loops failed."

The twilight of the chatbots

METR, the UK AI Security Institute, and Epoch all estimate AI capability on a better-than-exponential curve; Epoch measured Opus 4.7 working 14 hours to build a package that would take 2–17 weeks of human engineering for $251 in tokens. The dominant usage pattern is shifting from co-intelligence (chat with AI step-by-step) to delegation (assign work to agents). A joint OpenAI/academic study found 25% of OpenAI workers run 4+ agents weekly — and non-coders (legal, HR) adopted agents at nearly the same rate as engineers. A separate Claude Code study found user profession didn't predict success; domain expertise did — and expertise increased output per prompt.

"What actually mattered was not the profession of the user, but their expertise. The more domain experience someone had, the more successful they were in using Claude Code in that domain."

6 months to live for open models

White House discussions are reportedly underway to ban or indefinitely delay open-weight models above the capability of GPT-5.5 / Opus 4.8 / GLM-5.2 within ~6 months. The driver is dual: distillation concerns (Anthropic's campaign against Chinese labs using its API) and frontier-capability fears. Lambert argues the distillation debate is "largely regulatory capture at this point" — Anthropic gains substantially if Chinese competitors are banned, and could secure its own API if its technology is as powerful as claimed. GLM-5.2 (Z.ai, MIT-licensed) is the model most likely to trigger action; it's the first open model mixing with frontier closed models on agent leaderboards.

"Open models lack the central economic champion to represent the potential downside of action against them. … The most likely incoming action is to ban or indefinitely delay any open-weights model meaningfully above the capability level … in the range of GPT 5.5, Claude Opus 4.8, or GLM-5.2."

Trending Themes

  • Loop engineering is the new vocabulary, but the loop is the easy part. Every serious publication this week independently concludes the bottleneck is the system around the loop — verification, cost caps, trust accumulation, org absorption capacity.
  • The productivity-number reality check. Anecdotal 10x → company-internal 3x → telemetry-measured 24% PR lift. The number falls as the instrument sharpens. Engineering leaders should anchor on the 24% and treat the rest as ceiling cases.
  • Adoption is a social contagion, not a training program. Microsoft's data: skip-level peer usage (+216% odds) and direct-manager usage (+82% odds) are the strongest predictors. The manager is the ignition point — and managers are no more likely to adopt than anyone else.
  • Workforce bifurcation is now a measured variable. "Amplified by AI" vs. "shaken by AI" predicts career outlook more than seniority or company. Burnout up 11 points in a year.
  • Verification is the hidden cost. Loops that "work" can still cost $40K just to check; one team noted fully trusting results would push into "hundreds of thousands." Budget for checking, not just for running.
  • Open-weights option may not be durable. Policy action targeting frontier open models is reportedly within 6 months. Self-hosting strategies should not be single-threaded on Chinese open-weights.
  • From chatbots to agents, from prompting to managing. Mollick's framing: domain expertise (not engineering title) predicts agent success. The new core competency is managerial delegation to AI.
  • Coding agents replacing IDEs as the developer surface. AIEWF 2026 consensus: Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI, Cursor, Warp are the new daily interface, not Copilot-style autocomplete.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Make your engineering managers the first hands-on users — and measure adoption socially, not by mandate. The Microsoft telemetry study is unambiguous: an engineer whose direct manager uses Copilot CLI / Claude Code is 82% more likely to try it and 22% more likely to stick; skip-level peer usage drives 216% higher odds. Yet managers are statistically no more likely to adopt than any individual contributor. The single highest-leverage move this quarter is to require every engineering manager (and skip-level) to personally run at least one real coding-agent session per week, ship something from it, and visibly review the output. Pair this with the budget discipline Justin Johnson outlines: hard per-engineer token caps (Uber's $1,500/person cap after burning its annual budget in four months is the cautionary benchmark), a cheap-model-default policy with expensive models reserved for hard steps, and an explicit "cost to verify" line item in every agent-loop business case. The productivity ceiling is real — 24% measured PR lift is the floor to plan against, not 10x — but only the orgs whose managers light the match will see the social spread that gets them there.
