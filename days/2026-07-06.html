By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

Two major themes dominate this week's Substack content: the transition from chatbots to autonomous agents and the fundamental divide between AI builders and non-builders in leadership. Ethan Mollick's "The twilight of the chatbots" provides the clearest framing yet of the paradigm shift — AI is no longer a co-intelligent tool you prompt back-and-forth; it's becoming an autonomous worker that runs for hours without human intervention, with capability gains accelerating faster than exponential. His data point that Opus 4.7 built a software package requiring 2-17 weeks of human work in 14 hours at $251 in token costs is the kind of concrete metric engineering leaders need to internalize.

Meanwhile, Justin Johnson (Run Data Run) drops a sharp diagnosis: the real problem isn't AI capability but the asymmetric gap between those who have operated these systems and those who haven't. McKinsey's 40%+ project failure rate for agentic AI, he argues, isn't about overhyped tech — it's about leaders making deployment decisions without having personally crossed the experience gap. This connects directly to Ali El-Shayeb's practical piece on building agentic content supply chains and Colin Matthews' framework for PMs using AI tools — the organizations that succeed will be those whose leaders invest in firsthand operational experience, not tool procurement.

1. The twilight of the chatbots

AI is shifting from "co-intelligence" (back-and-forth prompting with a chatbot) to autonomous agents that work for hours on complex tasks with minimal human intervention. Capability is improving at a better-than-exponential rate as measured by METR, UK AISI, and Epoch. Open-weights Chinese models are on their own accelerating curve, lagging 6-12 months behind the US frontier.

"Another organization doing similar experiments, Epoch, recently found Opus 4.7, working on its own for 14 hours, was able to build a software package that would take 2-17 weeks of human engineering work (it cost $251 in tokens)."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: Your organization's AI strategy will fail not because the tech doesn't work, but because the people approving it haven't experienced what it can do firsthand. The most valuable investment isn't more tools — it's getting your leadership team across the experience gap.
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

2. The Gap, and the Book That Came Out of It

The AI debate isn't skeptics vs. believers — it's a structural gap between people who have built with these systems and people who haven't. McKinsey reports most orgs use AI but <40% capture value; Gartner expects 40%+ of agentic projects to be cancelled. These failure rates aren't evidence of overhype, but evidence that leaders without personal operational experience are making deployment decisions.

"The skeptic and the builder are not two sides of a balanced argument. They are the same function run on different inputs. If you have never seen a system go off, do real multi-step work, get something wrong, take your correction, and come back with it fixed, your mental model of AI is calibrated to the wrong tier."
By Colin MatthewsWhy read: The PM role is shifting from coordination to building. Leaders should expect every product IC to be able to prototype with code, not just write PRDs — this is how individual leverage increases 5-10x.
Author: Colin Matthews / Lenny's Newsletter

3. How top PMs increase their leverage with AI

A "three ladders of leverage" framework for how PMs use AI: (1) using AI tools for personal productivity in research and writing, (2) prototyping with real code using Codex/Claude Code/Cursor, and (3) shipping production changes via AI agents with MCP-based skill systems. The best PMs at top companies now prototype with real codebases and confidently run coding AI agents.

"For years, the PM job drifted toward coordinating and aligning people. That version of the role is fading. Today, the best PMs at the best companies are prototyping with real code, querying data conversationally with MCP, confidently running coding AI agents."
By Ali El-ShayebWhy read: For engineering leaders managing content or documentation pipelines: the headcount trap is real. Adding writers compounds management friction. The alternative is building agentic pipelines that treat content as a byproduct of your infrastructure, not human labor.
Author: Ali El-Shayeb / Islands

4. How to scale content creation without adding headcount

Moving from "assistant model" (human at center, AI helps write faster) to "autonomous supply chains" (multi-agent orchestration handles research, drafting, and structuring). The bottleneck isn't writing — it's data extraction, semantic mapping, and structural integrity. Internal knowledge ingestion from Slack and Fathom conversations replaces the need for subject-matter experts to write anything.

"Most startups are stuck in the assistant phase. They give a writer an AI tool and tell them to write faster. This is a mistake. Using AI as a drafting assistant still keeps the human at the center of the bottleneck."
By Jack ClarkWhy read: The agent scaffolding that worked for software is now being applied to physical systems. The "multi-agent beats single agent" finding reinforces that engineering leaders should plan for agent-team architectures, not solo agents.

5. Import AI 463: Self-improving robots; a 10k Chinese GPU cluster; and an elegiac essay for the human era

NVIDIA's ENPIRE system creates a self-improvement loop for physical robotics using the same agent scaffolding as coding agents — frontier coding agents can autonomously achieve 99% success rates on dexterous manipulation tasks. Also notable: multi-agent setups (8 agents) outperform single agents on these tasks, echoing findings from coding agent research. GPT-5.5/Codex and Opus 4.7/Claude Code trade off for best performance.

"Frontier coding agents can autonomously develop a policy to achieve a 99% success rate on challenging, dexterous manipulation tasks in the real world, such as PushT, organizing pins into a pin box, and using a cutter to cut a zip tie."

6. Claude Science and the boring 80 percent

Anthropic shipped Claude Science — a workbench and evaluation harness, not a model breakthrough. The model is the commodity layer; the harness, evaluation framework, and workflow scaffolding around it are the moat. The "boring 80 percent" — test harnesses, agent orchestration, human-in-the-loop infrastructure — is where real value is captured.

Trending Themes

  • From co-intelligence to autonomous agents. Multiple writers (Mollick, Johnson, El-Shayeb) independently converge on the same thesis — the era of "ask, check, repeat" AI usage is ending. The new paradigm is "set it running and verify output." This changes everything about how teams should structure work.
  • The experience gap is a leadership crisis. Justin Johnson's framing of the asymmetric gap between builders and non-builders is the most practically useful mental model this week. If your exec team hasn't personally used Claude Code or Codex for a nontrivial task, their AI strategy decisions are structurally compromised.
  • Harness > model. Both Johnson (Run Data Run) and Mollick make versions of this argument: model capability is improving so fast (and prices falling so fast) that picking the "best" model is a decision you'll remake four times a month. What compounds is the system around it — evaluations, tooling, workflows, feedback loops.
  • Multi-agent architectures beat single agents. Import AI's coverage of NVIDIA's ENPIRE robotics research echoes the coding agent literature: 8 agents exploring solution space in parallel beats 1 agent. Teams should design agent workflows to include parallel exploration and verification agents.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Schedule a "build week" for your senior leadership team. This week's most actionable insight across all posts is the experience gap (Johnson: "the boardroom argument stops being a debate about technology. It becomes a map of who in the room has ever operated the thing"). Set aside 3-5 days — not a demo, not a workshop — where every director and above personally builds something nontrivial with Claude Code or Codex, from conception through shipping. The goal isn't to make them engineers; it's to calibrate their mental models so they can accurately evaluate the agentic AI proposals coming across their desks. McKinsey's 40%+ failure rate on agentic projects will only improve when the people funding them have crossed the gap.
