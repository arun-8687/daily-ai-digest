By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

This week's Substack content is dominated by two themes: the evaluation bottleneck and the cultural/organizational dynamics of AI adoption. Justin Johnson's Run Data Run dropped two standout posts — one arguing that evaluation cost (not training cost) has become the binding constraint on AI progress, and another chronicling 14 months of real-world RAG iteration with refreshingly honest failure data. Meanwhile, Nathan Lambert's firsthand dispatch from Chinese AI labs offers a rare organizational lens on why China keeps closing the gap, while Jack Clark's Import AI makes a sober, data-driven case that fully automated AI R&D may arrive within two years.

The Pragmatic Engineer's paywalled Pulse edition covers the latest industry drama: Anthropic's apparent hostility toward developers (potentially capacity-constrained after the SpaceX compute deal), Amazon lifting its ban on Claude Code/Codex, and the emerging "small AI-forward teams" trend at Meta and Amazon. On the tooling frontier, Ben's Bites rounds up Codex's push for non-technical users and Claude Code's leaked architecture details.

Verdict: A high-signal week. The evaluation bottleneck thesis is the most actionable insight for engineering leaders — it reframes where to invest in AI infrastructure.

1. Evals Are the New Bottleneck

The cost of evaluating AI models has overtaken the cost of training them. A single standardized evaluation of nine frontier models cost $40K. Agent evaluations are combinatorial — CivBench costs $1,200 per match against Claude Opus 4.6. Meanwhile, headline benchmarks (MMLU, SWE-bench) are saturated above 90%, and the community is scrambling to build contamination-resistant replacements.

"The bottleneck moved while everyone was looking at GPUs. Most leadership teams haven't moved with it."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: The honest failure data is rare and valuable. Most teams treat RAG as a solve-once problem; this post shows it's a continuous engineering discipline where the asset (curated content) compounds but the exposure layer needs constant recalibration.
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

2. From the Vault, Literally

A 14-month chronicle of iterating a personal RAG system from naive keyword search over 500 markdown files to sophisticated hybrid retrieval + reranking across 28,264 notes. Key finding: "A weekend of compute, a hundred dollars of API spend, zero lift" — the fifth layer bought nothing, but swapping the embedder underneath delivered 18-point recall gains at zero cost.

"The vault is the brain. The retrieval layer is how the brain talks to the rest of the work. The exposure layer kept changing because the world kept giving it better ways to listen. The brain itself didn't move."
By Nathan LambertWhy read: If you're building an AI engineering team, this provides a concrete cultural template for accelerating model development velocity.
Author: Nathan Lambert / Interconnects AI

3. Notes from inside China's AI labs

Firsthand account from visiting leading Chinese AI labs. Lambert argues China's advantage isn't just compute or talent — it's organizational culture. Chinese labs integrate students as core contributors, suppress individual ego in favor of model-level optimization, and have a higher tolerance for non-flashy, meticulous work. In contrast, US labs struggle with internal politics — the Llama organization reportedly "collapsed under political weight."

"Where American researchers are obviously also brilliant at solving the individual components, there's more of a culture of speaking up for yourself in the U.S... Ego and desires for career advancement do get in the way of making the best models."
By Gergely OroszWhy read: Direct operational intelligence on the AI tools ecosystem — which tools major tech companies are using/banning, team sizing trends, and pricing shifts that affect every enterprise's AI tooling budget.
Author: Gergely Orosz / The Pragmatic Engineer

4. The Pulse: Did capacity shortages turn Anthropic hostile to devs?

Covers five major stories: (1) Anthropic's "dumber" model and Claude Code access removals may have been capacity-constrained after SpaceX compute deal; (2) Amazon finally lifts ban on Claude Code/Codex after trying to build its own (Kiro); (3) Meta forcefully assigns 20-40% of engineers to data labelling ahead of layoffs; (4) "Small AI-forward teams" trend — Meta/Amazon CEOs claim 5-10 devs outperform 50; (5) GitHub's Copilot pricing shifting to API token-based billing (roughly 3x increase on annual renewal).

"Meta and Amazon's CEOs say teams of 5-10 devs do better work than teams of 50. There are important caveats: it's unclear what they'll do with the 'excess,' and if it's limited to 'mechanical' work like rewrites."
By Jack ClarkWhy read: This reframes the AI developer productivity question from "how much faster can we code?" to "when do we become the supervisors of the code-writing system?" Long-term strategic implications for engineering workforce planning.
Author: Jack Clark / Import AI

5. Import AI 455: AI systems are about to start building themselves

A data-driven argument that there's a 60%+ chance of no-human-involved AI R&D by end of 2028. Clark traces evidence across SWE-bench scores, METR time horizons (coding agent tasks now span hours), automated ML research, and the commoditization of training. All pieces for automating AI development are now in place — the engineering components first, then potentially the creative research components.

"I reluctantly come to the view that there's a likely chance (60%+) that no-human-involved AI R&D happens by the end of 2028... The cost of knowing whether a model works has overtaken the cost of building one."
By swyx & AlessioWhy read: A concrete example of the capability wedge — AI's utility is unevenly distributed across tasks. Engineering leaders should identify their organization's "jagged frontier" (hard problems where AI excels) rather than measuring average productivity gains.
Author: swyx & Alessio / Latent Space

6. 🔬Doing Vibe Physics — Alex Lupsasca, OpenAI

Interview with theoretical physicist Alex Lupsasca (Breakthrough Prize winner) about how GPT-5.x reproduced one of his best papers in 30 minutes — work that took him "a very long time to come up with." Highlights the "jagged frontier" phenomenon: GPT-5.x reception was lukewarm on everyday tasks (email, coding) but transformative at the scientific frontier.

"I remember when GPT-5 came out… on Twitter, the reception was lukewarm. A lot of people were like, well, we expected a lot more, and it's not better at writing email. And I remember thinking, well, okay, GPT-3 could write email. How much better can it get at writing email? That's not the point. But at the science frontier, the capabilities were really taking off."

7. Codex is gaining steam

OpenAI is pushing Codex for non-technical users, allowing settings/plugin import from tools like Claude Cowork, and improving features for everyday work (slides, sheets). Also covers Grok 4.3 (cheaper than Sonnet 4.6), Entire's new git-sync and dispatches tools, and Honeycomb's upcoming event on observability for the agent era.

"OpenAI wants non-technical users to use Codex. They are making it easy for you to switch to Codex. You can now import settings, plugins, agents, project configuration and more into Codex."

Trending Themes

  • Evaluation is the new bottleneck. Multiple posts this week independently converge on this insight — the binding constraint on AI progress has shifted from training compute to evaluation cost and methodology.
  • Naive RAG → Sophisticated RAG. The "RAG is dead" headlines are misleading. What's dying is naive vector-dump RAG; hybrid retrieval + reranking on curated assets is thriving in production.
  • Small AI-forward teams. Meta and Amazon are publicly championing teams of 5-10 engineers amplified by AI tools over traditional 50-person teams. This has direct implications for org design.
  • The AI tooling ecosystem is fragmenting. Anthropic (Claude Code), OpenAI (Codex), Amazon (Kiro→lifting ban), and GitHub (Copilot pricing upheaval) are all moving in different directions. Vendor lock-in risk is rising.
  • China's organizational advantage. US labs may lead on raw innovation, but Chinese labs' cultural willingness to suppress ego for model-level optimization is a structural advantage in the iteration race.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Invest in evaluation infrastructure now. Based on the converging evidence this week, the smartest dollar your engineering org can spend is not on more GPUs or API credits — it's on building a robust, contamination-resistant evaluation framework for your AI agents. Start by identifying 3-5 real-world tasks your teams perform, instrument them with automated grading, and run weekly evals as your model/tool choices change. The orgs that build this muscle first will make faster, more confident decisions about which AI tools actually work for their specific workflows. The orgs that don't will be flying blind through the pricing upheaval and capability churn ahead.
