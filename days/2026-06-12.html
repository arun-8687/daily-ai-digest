By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

This week's dominant theme is the Mythos/Fable 5 model release and its immediate implications for engineering organizations. Three separate Substacks — Ethan Mollick, Justin Johnson (Run Data Run), and Nathan Lambert (Interconnects) — independently covered Anthropic's Claude Fable 5 launch, each from a different angle: Mollick focused on the qualitative experience of working with a dramatically more capable model, Johnson on how it collapsed the "private tool to public product" gap in a single session, and Lambert on the safety politics and trust implications. Taken together, they paint a picture of a model that genuinely feels like a step-change — but also one that introduces new governance questions for engineering leaders.

At the same time, Gergely Orosz at Pragmatic Engineer published a timely investigation into a growing counter-trend: companies cutting back on AI spend because they can't link the astronomical token usage to actual shipped product features. Uber's president, DoorDash's approach, and a traditional US retirement-savings company all feature. This is the most actionable piece for CIOs this week.

The Pulse: a trend of trying to cut back on AI spend within eng departments?

Gergely reports that companies across the spectrum are starting to ask hard questions about massive AI spend. Uber's president says the link between "25% of code commits via Claude Code" and "useful features shipped" is not there yet. OpenCode's founder reports every single inbound enterprise request is now about optimizing spend. DoorDash gives devs high monthly token limits but requires justification to exceed them. A traditional retirement-savings company imposes monthly Copilot token limits that auto-downgrade users to cheaper models.

"If you're not able to draw a direct line to [how many] useful features and functionality you're shipping to your users, that tradeoff [on AI spend] becomes harder to justify because AI is not free."
By Ethan MollickWhy read: This is the first hands-on account of what Mythos-class models actually feel like in practice — a shift from "AI as tool" to "AI as autonomous executor of multi-hour tasks."
Author: Ethan Mollick / One Useful Thing

What it feels like to work with Mythos

Mollick got early access to Claude 5 Fable (Anthropic's first Mythos-class public model). He documents it working autonomously for up to a dozen hours on multi-page specifications, spawning sub-agents to research travel times from 2,200+ flights while simultaneously coding the main application. His key observation: the feeling of using it is "somewhere between delightful and unnerving" because "I just asked for something and it happened."

"I just asked for something and it happened… Delightful because I just asked for something and it happened. And also unnerving because I just asked for something and it happened."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: This is a concrete productivity metric — what previously took 10x effort on Sonnet 4 and 2 nights on Opus, took one session on Fable 5. This type of before/after comparison is rare and valuable.
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

What one day with Fable 5 looks like for a builder

Johnson documents how Fable 5 compressed a weekend of tedious, dangerous work (sanitizing a personal finance app for public release) into a single working session. The model autonomously swept the entire git history for sensitive data, found brokerage positions in a forgotten test script, rewired infrastructure, and generated a public-facing version. His framing: the gap between "a tool that works for me" and "a tool anyone can run" used to be a weekend nobody takes.

"The gap between a private tool that works and a public one anyone can run used to be a weekend nobody takes. That is why good private tools die private."
By Gergely OroszWhy read: Shows the gap between AI IDE hype and execution reality. Google's product confusion is a cautionary tale for any org betting on a single-vendor AI toolchain.

The Pulse: Antigravity 2.0 takes 'IDE' out of its new IDE

Gergely's hands-on review of Google's Antigravity 2.0 launch is brutal: the upgrade process is broken, the "Agent Manager" concept is unintuitive even to its own creators, the product cannibalizes its predecessor, and it burns through expensive Gemini token quotas in minutes. Even Google's own developers reportedly dual-wield Antigravity 2.0 with other IDEs.

"The upgrade feels rushed, sloppy, and poorly thought out… Google looks indecisive about what to do with the IDE part of Antigravity."

Claude Fable 5 and new AI safety fables

Lambert analyzes the Fable 5 release from a safety and trust angle — Anthropic deployed silent model modification (downgrading certain prompts to Opus 4.8 without telling users) alongside the capabilities leap. He notes Fable 5 is "definitely the smartest model available to the general public" at only 2x the price of current Opus, but the safety measures meaningfully change access to frontier AI.

"The unevenly applied safety policies that Anthropic have rolled out are on track to become a classic cautionary fable in how narrow and self-fulfilling notions of safety and control rarely work out."

Trending Themes

  • Fable 5 dominance: 4+ major Substacks covered the same release from different angles — unusual coordination suggests it's the most consequential AI release in months.
  • AI cost scrutiny is mainstreaming: The Pragmatic Engineer's reporting on orgs pulling back on AI spend echoes into discussions at Uber, OpenCode, and beyond. This is now a board-level conversation.
  • The "private-to-public" gap is closing: Both Mollick and Johnson independently observed that the new class of models turns vague prompts into polished, production-ready outputs with minimal iteration — a genuine qualitative shift.
  • IDE confusion: Google's Antigravity 2.0 misfire and the broader uncertainty around what an "AI IDE" should be suggests the market is still searching for the right product paradigm.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Run an AI spend audit this week — before your CFO does. Use the framework from the Pragmatic Engineer's reporting: for each AI tool license or API subscription, ask "what shipped feature can I trace back to this spend?" If the answer is vague, implement model routing (e.g., cheap models for boilerplate, premium models for complex reasoning) and establish per-developer token budgets with justification gates. Several companies (DoorDash, unnamed traditional firms) have already implemented models worth copying. The Fable 5 era makes this even more urgent — the new model class is 2x the price of Opus, and without governance, token consumption will explode faster than feature output.
