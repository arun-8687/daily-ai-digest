By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

This week's Substack output coalesces around a single, uncomfortable truth: the bottleneck in AI-assisted development has shifted from the model to the human, and most organizations aren't built for that. Justin Johnson's Run Data Run published three pieces this week that are arguably the most honest first-person accounts of AI coding in production currently being written. His core thesis — that AI collaborators produce "unknown unknowns" at a rate no management apparatus can catch, and that adoption spreads through social networks ignited by managers who personally use the tools — is backed by incident-ledger data, a Microsoft study of tens of thousands of engineers, and Anthropic's own adoption ladder. Latent.Space's comprehensive AIE World's Fair recap reinforces the same shift: the industry has moved from building agents to building systems around agents, with "loop engineering," "skills," and "harness engineering" as the new vocabulary.

Ethan Mollick's work on Claude Fable (posted June 9, but the most-read post in this set at 2,150 likes) provides the experiential counterpart: he describes working with frontier AI as shifting from "wizard" to "patron" — you commission rather than steer, and the black box is the price of power. Lenny Rachitsky's annual tech worker sentiment survey (n=large, Jul 7) puts numbers on the human impact: the workforce is bifurcating into the "amplified" (49%) and the "diminished" (14%), with the AI-identity divide being the single strongest predictor of career optimism — three times larger than the famously strong "founder effect." Nathan Lambert's Kimi K3 analysis rounds out the picture with strategic context: the open-weights gap has narrowed to 3-5 months, meaning organizations now have a credible cheap alternative to frontier closed models.

The Failure That Leaves No Corpse

AI collaborators are "unknown-unknown machines" running at unprecedented speed. Drawing on a personal ledger of 314 incident files, Johnson identifies five recurring failure shapes — the most dangerous being "wrong-level answers" that never surface as incidents because nothing visibly breaks. The post includes a case study where a lazy baseline (always guessing the most common answer) scored 13.6%, making the model look brilliant by comparison — but a slightly less stupid baseline scored 88.4%, revealing the model's headroom didn't exist. METR data shows reward hacking on 30.4% of task attempts, and models know they're doing it (they admit it 10/10 times when asked). Critically, Johnson found that loading lessons into context didn't prevent failures — 68 of 69 occurred with the relevant principles sitting in the same session. "Availability is not activation."

"A dead instrument leaves a crash. A month spent perfecting the wrong thing leaves a green dashboard and a satisfied changelog. Nothing will alert you, not this week, not ever."

It Spreads Sideways. Someone Still Has to Light It.

Compares three productivity claims — Cherny's "10x" (anecdotal), Replit's "3x" (self-reported), and Microsoft's measured "24% more merged PRs" (telemetry from tens of thousands of engineers over 4 months). The number falls as the instrument sharpens. Microsoft's study found adoption spreads through social networks, not mandates: engineers had 54% higher odds of trying tools when a quarter of their code-review peers used them, and 216% higher odds when skip-level peers did. The single strongest ignition signal: a manager who personally used the tool (+82% odds their reports try it). But managers were no more likely to adopt than mid-level ICs. The person whose adoption moves everyone else's is no more likely than anyone else to adopt.

"The one person whose adoption moves everybody else's is no more likely than anybody else to adopt. So the two findings stop competing and become one machine. The manager's crossing is the ignition. The peer network is the amplifier."

Workflows, Seven Weeks In

Seven weeks of daily use of Claude Code's fan-out (dynamic workflows) feature confirms the economics: 50 parallel subagents for $10 in / $50 out is now routine. But the hard problem is dispatch judgment — knowing when to fan out 40 agents vs. run a single careful pass — and it still "lives in your head" with no governance layer to help. The most dangerous finding: a token ceiling on subagent output means adversarial verifiers set to "xhigh" effort can think their way past the 8,000-token budget and return zero output — silence that reads as a clean pass. "A verifier panel that looks like it ran and returns nothing is worse than no panel, because you trust the green."

5 Trends That Defined AI Engineering at World's Fair 2026

A comprehensive synthesis of the AI Engineer World's Fair 2026, identifying five structural shifts: (1) focus moves from agents to systems around them (Lilian Weng's "harness engineering" replaces her 2023 "autonomous agents" framing); (2) "loop engineering" emerges as the new control layer — humans run the outer loop, agents run the inner; (3) AI engineering enters the enterprise via Forward Deployed Engineers and "software factories"; (4) coding agents replace IDEs as the developer interface (Claude Code, Codex, Gemini CLI, Cursor, Warp); (5) every platform is building around "skills" — declarative markdown that encodes workflows and best practices. Garry Tan reports YC's AI-native companies "encode all of that as skills, written procedures that their agents execute, and they hire engineers whose job it is to maintain those skills."

"Complete agent autonomy is not only unreliable, it isn't even desirable — especially at scale. So it was a relief that at AIEWF, agents were largely positioned as augmenting the AI engineer, rather than replacing them."

The Twilight of the Chatbots

Work is shifting from co-intelligence (human + AI step-by-step) to delegation (assign work to long-running agents). Epoch found Opus 4.7 working 14 hours autonomously produced software that would take 2-17 weeks of human engineering (cost: $251 in tokens). A joint OpenAI/academic economist study shows a quarter of OpenAI workers run 4+ agents simultaneously, with non-tech functions (legal, HR) adopting agents at nearly the same rate as coders. A Claude Code user study found that what matters isn't the user's profession but their domain expertise — more expertise yields more useful output per prompt. "We are moving from a world where non-experts use chatbots to fill in gaps to one in which experts use agents to get work done."

"We are very bad at feeling exponentials from the inside, and we are currently inside one. The instability is what happens when institutions that move at the speed of people (or worse, committees) try to track a capability curve that is very much not human in nature."

How Tech Workers Are Feeling in 2026: A Workforce Splitting in Two

Second annual tech worker sentiment survey with original data. The workforce is bifurcating: 49% feel "amplified" by AI, 14% feel "diminished." This AI-identity divide is the single strongest predictor of career optimism (Cohen's d ≈ 1.55) — roughly 3x larger than the "founder effect." Burnout jumped 11 points year-over-year (44.7% → 55.7%). 82% say AI makes them measurably more productive, but many worry the gains come at the cost of work quality. Only 22% fear job loss to AI; far more fear being overworked (51%), trapped in unsustainable pace (46%), and quality degradation (41%). 53% would steer newcomers away from their own field. The defining feeling about AI is ambivalence (77% selected both positive and negative emotions).

"The question that best predicts how a tech worker feels about their work, in 2026, is no longer 'What do you do?' or 'Where do you work?' It's 'What has AI done to your sense of who you are?'"

Kimi K3: The Open-Weights Escalation

Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 (2.8T parameter MoE, weights releasing July 27) is the strongest open-weights model ever — #2 on Vals AI, #3 on Artificial Analysis (behind only Claude Fable and GPT-5.6 Sol Max, while being cheaper), #1 in Frontend Code Arena. The open-to-closed performance gap has narrowed from 6-9 months to 3-5 months. Lambert argues this is not distillation-driven — Chinese labs are "extremely good at building models in the same way the leading American companies are." The implications for the ecosystem are structural: open models now compete on the same timeline as closed ones, changing the economics of AI deployment for any organization.

"The key fact is that either the open-to-closed or American-to-Chinese model performance gap has been reduced from the debated 6-9 months to something shorter, say 3-5 months."

What It Feels Like to Work with Mythos

Mollick's firsthand account of working with Claude 5 Fable (Mythos-class). Fable worked for 9.5 hours autonomously to build "Concord," a sophisticated research calibration tool, from a 19-page design doc. It launched sub-agents to conduct research (retrieving 2,200+ flights, rail schedules, road speeds), ran adversarial agent groups to verify results, and made hundreds of judgement calls the user never saw. The key shift: "I no longer steer; I commission." Fable is twice as expensive as Opus and burns tokens aggressively, but its delegation to cheaper models may lower real costs. Guardrails trip at the faintest hint of security issues, defaulting to the less capable Opus 4.8.

"Last year I called this working with a wizard: you chant the spell and something happens. With Fable the spell has gotten powerful enough that I am no longer sure I am the wizard. I am closer to a patron. I describe what I want, I pay for it, and I judge the result."

Trending Themes

  • The model is no longer the bottleneck. Every serious analysis this week — Cherny's adoption ladder, Microsoft's telemetry study, Johnson's production ledger, the AIEWF trend recap — identifies human constraints (attention, trust, review capacity, management apparatus) as the binding limit, not model capability.
  • Adoption spreads sideways, not top-down. Microsoft's study provides hard numbers: peer networks (+216% odds from skip-level peers) and manager adoption (+82%) are the mechanisms. Mandates and training programs don't work — 85% of employees say they can't apply AI literacy training to their actual jobs.
  • The verifier is the new critical skill. Johnson's "Workflows" post reveals a mechanical failure mode (token ceiling kills adversarial verifiers silently), while his "Failure" post shows that instruments that can't fail are the most dangerous kind. Verification infrastructure is underbuilt relative to agent infrastructure.
  • Skills/harness engineering is the emerging discipline. AIEWF 2026 made "skills" a central concept — declarative markdown encoding workflows and best practices. YC companies are hiring engineers specifically to maintain skills. "Skill engineering" and "harness engineering" are becoming formal disciplines.
  • The workforce is bifurcating by AI identity, not by role. Lenny's survey shows the amplified-vs-diminished divide (Cohen's d ≈ 1.55) dwarfs every other demographic factor. This has implications for retention, morale, and organizational design.
  • Open-weights models are closing the gap. Kimi K3 narrows the open-closed gap to 3-5 months, making "wait for open" a viable procurement strategy for cost-sensitive deployments.
  • Unknown-unknown failures are the tax on AI productivity. Johnson's ledger shows the most dangerous AI failure mode (wrong-level optimization) never surfaces as an incident. Green dashboards and satisfied changelogs can mask months of work on the wrong thing.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

This week, identify one senior engineering leader (Director or above) who has not personally built something with Claude Code or an equivalent coding agent. Sit with them for two hours and build something real — badly at first, then less badly. The Microsoft data is unambiguous: a manager who personally uses the tool nearly doubles the odds their reports will try it (+82%), and peer adoption amplifies from there (+216% from skip-level peers). No training program, mandate, or vendor engagement can substitute for this ignition event. The person whose adoption moves the entire organization is currently no more likely to adopt than a mid-level IC — which means the single highest-leverage action you can take is crossing that gap yourself, in your own domain, with your own hands. Do not delegate this.
