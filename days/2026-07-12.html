By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

This week's Substack coverage reveals two converging themes: the AI coding cost crisis is real and accelerating, and the workforce is splitting into those amplified by AI and those made anxious by it. Gergely Orosz's analysis of Cursor's internal data provides a rare data-driven look at how AI coding tools actually behave in production — the median dev generates ~700 LOC/week with Cursor, but top 1% users produce 30–40K LOC/week (equivalent to ~45 median devs). The shocking finding: 90% of token spend is on input tokens (reading codebase context), not output tokens. With caching, Cursor spends only 0.6% of tokens on actual code generation.

Meanwhile, the productivity‑ROI debate is peaking. Uber's president publicly stated the link between AI token spend and shipped features "is not there yet," and Pragmatic Engineer sources reveal companies are imposing monthly token limits and forcing developers onto cheaper models. Import AI reports Fable 5 hit 16.1% success on the Remote Labor Index (up from 2.5% in October 2025) and wrote an 18.7× faster GPU kernel — concrete capability metrics but also a harbinger of rising inference costs. Ethan Mollick's "twilight of the chatbots" thesis argues the paradigm is shifting from co-intelligent chatbots to autonomous agents that work for hours without human intervention, a transition that fundamentally changes how engineering teams should budget and evaluate tooling.

1. The Pulse: Interesting AI coding stats from Cursor

Cursor's two-year aggregate data reveals a 10:1 input-to-output token ratio (90% of tokens read codebase context), median devs generate 700 LOC/week, p99 users generate 30–40K LOC/week. Without caching, costs would be 10× higher. Opus 4.7 is 10× more expensive than Cursor's Composer 2.5, and almost half of AI-generated changes are accepted without manual review.

"The top 1% of users generate around 30-40K lines of code per week! That's the equivalent of what ~45 'median' devs generate in the same period."

3. The twilight of the chatbots

AI capability gains are accelerating at better-than-exponential rates. Opus 4.7 working autonomously for 14 hours built a software package requiring 2–17 weeks of human work ($251 in tokens). The dominant AI usage paradigm is shifting from co-intelligent chatbots (human-in-the-loop) to autonomous agents that execute multi-hour tasks without intervention. Open-weights Chinese models lag 6–12 months behind but are on their own exponential curve.

"Another organization doing similar experiments, Epoch, recently found Opus 4.7, working on its own for 14 hours, was able to build a software package that would take 2-17 weeks of human engineering work (it cost $251 in tokens)."

4. Why AI Infrastructure must evolve for Agent Experience

Cloud infrastructure was designed for humans who can read docs and debug YAML. Agents cannot do that — they need programmatic infra with fast iteration loops, sandboxes, and self-serve compute. Modal's $355M Series C and Agent Cloud thesis: infra that agents themselves can operate is the next platform shift.

"The old infra stack was designed for a human who could read docs, reason through YAML, and understand dashboards. However, agents don't have that luxury."

5. How tech workers are feeling in 2026: a workforce splitting in two

Large-scale survey (2nd annual) reveals bifurcation: one half feels amplified by AI, the other shaken by it. 82% say AI makes them measurably more productive, but burnout surged from 44.7% to 55.7% in one year. Only 22% worry about losing their job to AI; 51% fear being expected to do more for the same pay. Quality concerns are widespread.

"The workforce is bifurcating into two realities. One half feels amplified by AI — more capable, more confident, more excited than they've been in their entire career. The other half feels shaken by it — less sure of their value."

Trending Themes

  • AI cost governance is the new priority. Multiple pubs (Pragmatic Engineer, AI Supremacy, The AI Corner) all hit the same note: companies are hitting a wall where token spend is growing faster than demonstrable output. Smart model routing, caching strategies, and developer-level token budgets are emerging as the key countermeasures.
  • The agent paradigm shift is real but messy. Both Mollick and Import AI independently document the shift from co-intelligent chatbots to autonomous agents. Modal's thesis that infra must be redesigned for agents, not humans, is a concrete architectural implication. But Ben's Bites and Lenny's survey both highlight that current harnesses are immature and the "productivity" number hides quality concerns.
  • Input tokens are the hidden cost driver. Cursor's data showing 90% of token spend on reading codebase context (plus cache reads) flips conventional wisdom. The 10:1 read-to-write ratio for AI tokens mirrors the classic human reading-to-writing ratio — but it means AI cost optimization requires context caching and prompt compression, not just model selection.
  • The workforce is bifurcating. Lenny's survey and Pragmatic Engineer's sourcing both suggest that AI is creating a split between devs who feel empowered and those who feel threatened. Burnout is up 11 points. This is an organizational risk that technology alone cannot solve.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Audit your AI coding token spend against output metrics this week. Follow the Pragmatic Engineer's sourcing: ask your senior engineering leaders "how many projects that were on the cutting room floor got moved above the line because of AI productivity gains?" If the answer is unclear, implement smart model routing (e.g., Factory Router, Not Diamond, or LiteLLM auto-routing) before imposing blanket downgrades. Separately, pilot an internal knowledge-sharing series on efficient AI usage — the companies seeing the best results (DoorDash, per the Pragmatic Engineer) give devs autonomy to manage their own token budgets combined with peer-led efficiency sessions. The cost crisis is real, but blunt top-down restrictions destroy the innovation upside.
