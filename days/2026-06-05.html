By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

Today's scan reveals a field in rapid transition. The dominant narrative across the highest-signal Substack publications is that the model itself is no longer the frontier — the unit of value has shifted to the orchestration layer, the skill files, and the workflows running on top of LLMs. Three publications in particular delivered standout content: Run Data Run's Justin Johnson published two deeply original posts today dissecting self-editing skills and autonomous agent compounding; Ethan Mollick announced his new book "Co-Existence" with a candid accounting of how he does (and doesn't) use AI as a writer; and Latent Space's interview with GitHub's Kyle Daigle revealed that agentic coding has driven GitHub commits from 1 billion in all of 2025 to a run rate of 14 billion in 2026 — a 14x surge that is straining platform infrastructure in ways most engineering leaders haven't modeled.

A second theme: the gap between individual developer productivity gains (20-25%) and actual shipping velocity. Islands' post on the hidden costs of AI coding tools documents a case where 15 hrs/week saved per developer created 12 hrs/week of additional architecture review overhead — yielding only 3 net hours. The lesson is that local optimization without systemic redesign creates hidden taxes.

1. She Already Built It

Johnson set out to add federated learning to ARIA (his autonomous research agent). She had already designed and built it — three months earlier, without being asked. The post is a raw look at what compounding autonomous research looks like when the agent generates, scores, and executes its own hypotheses on a continuous loop with no human in the loop.

"The paper is a photograph of a system running. What it can't show is what's left in the corpus after the shutter clicks."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: Engineering leaders need to understand that the model is rented — the skill is what your team owns. This reframes AI adoption governance from "which model" to "how do we curate, version, and govern our skill library."
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

2. The skill that edits its own instructions

The "skill" (a written Markdown file of procedural instructions for an AI assistant) has become the new atomic unit of work. Johnson documents the ecosystem-wide shift toward self-editing skills that improve their own instructions after each run. There are now 1.6 million skill files on public GitHub, up from 790,000 six months ago. The open standard runs across Codex, Cursor, Copilot, and 30+ tools.

"The model is rented, and it gets swapped for a better one every few months. The skill is the part you own."
By Ethan MollickWhy read: Mollick is the most credible academic voice on AI productivity. His framework for when to use AI (and when not to) — including using fewer em-dashes to "prove the text was human" — is the kind of honest friction analysis that cuts through the hype.
Author: Ethan Mollick / One Useful Thing

3. Co-Existence and the End of Co-Intelligence

He cites Anthropic's data that AI now writes 80% of its code with each developer shipping 8x more, and another study showing 17x more code being written with coding agents.

"What is happening in coding is going to be happening in many fields."

A detailed technical breakdown of Anthropic's Opus 4.8 + Dynamic Workflows release. The key change: pricing dropped from $30/$150 per million tokens to $10/$50, 2.5x faster, with adaptive reasoning (model decides thinking depth) and a new JavaScript orchestration primitive set (agent(), parallel(), pipeline(), phase()). "One careful pass is no longer the default."

"Anthropic just repositioned the unit of agentic work, and the pricing finally allows what the tooling implies."

6. The Atomic Unit of Work Just Changed

The frontier has moved from the model to the "skill" — a named, versioned, governed piece of procedural knowledge that a frontier model executes with judgment. Johnson runs ~70 such skills across 4 machines. A skill is a Markdown file in a folder — "a Word doc that happens to run." Unlike traditional software, branches don't need to be enumerated in advance; the model supplies runtime judgment.

"The model commoditized. The frontier moved to the unit of work running on top of it, and that unit can now reason."
By Ethan MollickWhy read: The distinction between "AI as answer-giver" and "AI as thinking partner" is the most important leadership decision for AI adoption in engineering organizations. The data is now clear that the wrong implementation approach produces negative returns.
Author: Ethan Mollick / One Useful Thing

7. Choosing to Stay Human

A nuanced look at the difference between using AI to shortcut thinking vs. help thinking. Cites two overlapping studies: one where ChatGPT-using students did better on homework but worse on tests (short-circuiting learning), and another where an AI tutor that personalized problems boosted scores by 0.15 standard deviations (equivalent to 6-9 months of additional schooling).

"Using AI as a default, or even worse, without thinking at all... short-circuiting effort, you short-circuit learning."
By Ali El-ShayebWhy read: This is the clearest articulation I've seen of why individual productivity metrics don't translate to organizational throughput. Essential reading before making AI tool procurement decisions.
Author: Ali El-Shayeb / Islands

8. Your team is coding 25% faster. Why is production slower?

A documented case study of the AI productivity paradox. A Series B company found their AI coding assistant saved developers 15 hours/week but created 12 additional hours/week for the architecture team reviewing generated code for pattern compliance. Net gain: 3 hours. Coding is only ~40% of the software delivery lifecycle — optimizing one part without addressing bottlenecks in testing, review, deployment, and monitoring produces local gains but system-level disappointment.

"Faster coding doesn't automatically mean faster shipping. For many organizations, AI coding tools are creating a hidden tax that's offsetting the productivity gains."

Trending Themes

  • Skills as the new atomic unit. Three separate posts this week converged on the idea that "skills" (written procedural knowledge files) — not models, not agents, not prompts — are the durable unit of work that teams should invest in. The ecosystem has already produced 1.6 million public skill files.
  • The productivity paradox is real. Multiple sources confirm that individual developer speed gains (20-50%) don't translate to shipping velocity without systemic re-architecture of testing, review, and deployment pipelines.
  • Agentic code volume is straining infrastructure. GitHub's 14x commit surge, the Primeagen's "10s of billions of lines of code that will never see the light of a CPU," and the 1400% growth in agentic coding all point to an infrastructure reckoning.
  • Self-improving systems are becoming practical. From ARIA (autonomous research agent) to self-editing skills to Dynamic Workflows, the pattern of systems that improve their own instructions and orchestration is moving from research demo to production reality.
  • Price drops enable architectural shifts. Anthropic's 3x Opus pricing cut and 2.5x speedup (from $30/$150 to $10/$50 per M tokens) changes the economics of multi-step agentic workflows.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Start cataloging your "skills" today. Before investing in the next model upgrade or agent framework, run an audit: what procedural knowledge does your engineering team currently hold in documents, runbooks, READMEs, and tribal memory? Convert the highest-value 5-10 into the open skill format (Markdown files in a standard directory structure) that now runs across Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, and Copilot. This costs nothing, is portable across any model provider, and creates the institutional memory that survives model swaps. The teams that build a curated, versioned skill library will compound their AI productivity; the teams that chase model upgrades will tread water.
