By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

The dominant theme this week across Substack publications is the maturation of agentic coding workflows — specifically, the shift from "prompting models" to "engineering systems around models." Multiple high-signal posts converge on the same insight: the frontier of AI developer productivity is no longer about better prompts, but about designing reliable loops, managing context windows intelligently, and building harnesses that keep humans in control while letting agents do the heavy lifting.

A second major theme is the bifurcation of the tech workforce. Lenny Rachitsky and Noam Segal's survey of tech worker sentiment reveals a workforce splitting in two: 49% feel "amplified" by AI while 14% feel "destabilized" or "diminished," with this divide being the single strongest predictor of career outlook — more than title, tenure, or company size. Burnout jumped 11 points year-over-year (44.7% → 55.7%), and 82% report AI makes them more productive, but many worry the gains come at the cost of work quality and worker sharpness.

Meanwhile, Jack Clark's Import AI reports on the MirrorCode benchmark from Epoch and METR, which shows AI systems can now reimplement complex software programs (61k+ lines of code) from scratch using only CLI access — with Claude Opus 4.7 completing tasks in 14 hours that would take humans 2-17 weeks. This is a concrete data point on the rapidly expanding ceiling of autonomous AI programming capability.

What is "loop engineering"?

"Loop engineering" — designing autonomous agent loops instead of manually prompting — has become the trending new workflow paradigm after Anthropic's Boris Cherny (Claude Code creator) said "I don't prompt Claude anymore. I have loops running that prompt Claude... My job is to write loops." Gergely traces the concept from Geoffrey Huntley's "Ralph Wiggum" bash loops (a year ago) through the /goal command shipping in Codex, Claude Code, and Hermes by May 2026. He collects real-world examples from ~210 developers: agents that auto-open PRs for Sentry errors, fix flaky tests nightly, triage incidents before humans page in, and babysit e2e test runs. But he also surfaces honest pushback: agents drift, "tokenmaxxing" gets expensive at API prices, and distinguished engineer Max Kanat-Alexander suggests loops may have been a temporary hack while harnesses caught up.

"I don't prompt Claude anymore. I have loops running that prompt Claude and figuring out what to do. My job is to write loops." — Boris Cherny, creator of Claude Code

Context engineering with Dex Horthy

Dex Horthy, who coined "context engineering," shares 12 hard-won lessons from building AI-assisted dev systems. Key findings: (1) Shipping unread AI code spells disaster within months — his team tried it and had to throw out the entire system after 4 months when production broke and no human could find the root cause. (2) Today's coding models are likely trained to optimize SWE-bench, which means they make codebases worse over time because bad architecture can't be caught by unit tests. (3) The "dumb zone" starts at ~300-400K tokens for 1M context models — beyond that, models start doing "increasingly stupid things like deleting your .env file." (4) Three viable "software factory" models: "turn the lights off" (failed for Dex), "read and review all code" (30-50% productivity lift), and "find leverage but keep people in the loop" (2-3x faster).

How tech workers are feeling in 2026: a workforce splitting in two

A large-scale survey reveals the tech workforce is bifurcating: 49% feel "amplified" by AI, 27.4% "redefined," 13.9% "destabilized," and 5% "diminished." AI-identity stance is the single strongest predictor of career outlook — more than title, tenure, or company size. Burnout rose from 44.7% to 55.7% in one year. Only 22% worry about losing their job to AI; far more worry about being expected to do more for the same pay (51%), getting trapped in unsustainable pace (46%), and work quality declining (41%). 82% say AI makes them more productive, but many worry "the gains are coming at the cost of the sharpness of the work and the worker." 53% would steer newcomers away from their field.

5 Trends That Defined AI Engineering at World's Fair 2026

From the AI Engineer World's Fair, five trends emerged: (1) The focus shifts from agents to systems around them — Lilian Weng's new essay argues harness engineering (workflows, context, permissions, evals) matters as much as the agent. (2) Loop engineering is the new control layer — engineers put themselves in an "outer loop" overseeing autonomous "inner loops." (3) AI engineering enters the enterprise via Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) — Cursor's FDEs deploy "software factories" with "strict ROI" for customers. (4) Coding agents replace IDEs as the developer interface — tools like Claude Code, Codex, and Cursor can explore codebases, modify files, run tests, and iterate. (5) Every platform is building around "skills" — Markdown files that encode workflows and best practices, with Google DeepMind's Philipp Schmid saying "agents are just files."

By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Pilot a "leverage-first" AI coding workflow with guardrails. Based on Dex Horthy's proven framework: designate one team to adopt the "find leverage, keep people in the loop" model — invest more time in design and architecture decisions (where models are weak), let agents generate implementation code, and don't insist on reviewing every line. Expect a 2-3x productivity improvement. But mandatorily pair this with: (1) periodic architecture reviews by senior engineers who can detect model-induced codebase degradation, (2) context engineering training so devs know when they've hit the "dumb zone" (~300-400K tokens), and (3) nightly agent loops for low-risk tasks (flaky test fixes, Sentry error triage) that open PRs for human morning review. Measure both velocity and code quality metrics over 90 days before scaling.
