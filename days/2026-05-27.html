By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

Two major themes dominate today's Substack content: the commoditization of the model layer and the emergence of "skills" as the new atomic unit of work (Justin Johnson), and the growing tension between AI acceleration and cognitive atrophy (Ethan Mollick). Both threads converge on a single insight for engineering leaders: the frontier has moved from raw model capability to the procedural infrastructure — skills, workflows, governance, and interfaces — that determine whether AI deployment actually produces durable value.

On the infrastructure side, Daytona's 74% MoM growth and Railway's "agent-native cloud" thesis signal that a new platform layer is forming specifically to give AI agents composable, stateful compute environments. Ali El-Shayeb's field report from Web Summit Vancouver underscores that most founders are still in the "AI as marketing label" phase, while the real distinction is between generative (gives you a draft) and agentic (does the work). The signal-to-noise ratio across Substack this week is high — several posts offer genuinely novel frameworks rather than recycled hype.

1. The Atomic Unit of Work Just Changed

The model has become a commodity. The new frontier is the "skill" — a named, versioned, governed piece of procedural knowledge that a frontier model executes with judgment. Johnson describes running ~70 such skills across 4 machines, each being a folder with a Markdown file. Three independent research groups (SkillOpt, SkillsVote, SkillOS) all converged on the same abstraction in the same month. A skill is "a Word doc that happens to run."

"A tool is the atomic unit of computation. A skill is the atomic unit of process, and unlike a function, it reasons. A library where you can only add is one you can't reason about in six months."
By Ethan MollickWhy read: Engineering leaders designing AI adoption programs must build in friction that prevents cognitive surrender — otherwise they risk eroding the exact judgment and expertise their teams need to develop.
Author: Ethan Mollick / One Useful Thing

2. Choosing to Stay Human

Mollick draws on two education studies with overlapping research teams. In one, Turkish high school students using plain ChatGPT did better homework but underperformed at test time — AI as shortcut undermined learning. In the other, Taipei students using an AI tutor that personalized problem sequences scored 0.15σ higher (≈6-9 months of schooling). The difference is subtle but decisive: AI that pushes you to think vs. AI that thinks for you. Mollick calls this "cognitive surrender" and notes that agentic systems are especially dangerous because they "just do stuff."

"The most important thing we can do is keep asking what to hand over and what to keep for ourselves… and not expect anyone, including the AI, to answer that for us."
By Ali El-ShayebWhy read: Helps leaders distinguish between surface-level AI adoption (adding ChatGPT) and real operational transformation (deploying systems that run without human-in-the-loop).
Author: Ali El-Shayeb / Islands

3. The question 90 founders asked us at web summit Vancouver

At Web Summit Vancouver, the recurring question from founders was "Wait — you actually build agentic systems? Like, real ones?" The word "AI" has lost all categorical meaning — it covers everything from a GPT wrapper to a multi-agent production system. The critical distinction: "Generative AI gives you a draft. Agentic AI does the work." Three patterns emerged: founders are post-tool but pre-system; operators want autonomy, not assistance; trust is the entire gate.

"Generative AI gives you a draft. Agentic AI does the work. If a system writes you something and you read, edit, and post it — that's generative. You're still doing the job."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: Engineering leaders should stop obsessing over which foundation model to use and start investing in the surrounding infrastructure — skill libraries, evaluation frameworks, and governance systems.
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

4. The Model Is No Longer the Frontier

The first ACM conference on agentic systems convened with almost none of the papers touching the model itself. The hard problems have moved to everything that makes a model useful in practice: evals, skills, governance, safety, and orchestration. The model layer is commoditizing; the value capture is shifting upward.

"The model commoditized. The frontier moved to the unit of work running on top of it, and that unit can now reason."

Daytona's CEO makes the case that AI agents need more than code execution boxes — they need composable computers with stateful sandboxes, instant startup, dynamic resource scaling, and infrastructure that survives workloads going from zero to 100,000 CPUs. "The end of localhost" thesis, which Burazin has been arguing since 2010, is finally playing out because agents can't be paused by closing a laptop.

Trending Themes

  • The "skill" abstraction is crystallizing. Three independent research groups (Microsoft SkillOpt, SkillsVote, SkillOS) all published on the same concept in the same month — a trend Justin Johnson calls "the simultaneity is the signal." This is the most important emerging pattern for how AI work will be structured.
  • Cognitive surrender is the hidden cost of agentic systems. Mollick's research-backed warning that AI that "just does stuff" undermines learning and judgment directly contradicts the frictionless-agent narrative. Leaders need to design for deliberate use, not just productivity gains.
  • "Generative vs. Agentic" is the new meaningful taxonomy. Ali El-Shayeb's framing — "Generative AI gives you a draft. Agentic AI does the work" — is the clearest articulation yet of the categorical difference that most organizations still don't distinguish.
  • Agent infrastructure is booming. Daytona (74% MoM, 850K runs/day) and Railway (3M users, 100K signups/week) both signal that a new platform layer specifically for agent workloads is emerging rapidly.
  • The model IS the commodity. Every post this week reinforces that the differentiator is no longer model capability but the procedural, governance, and infrastructure layer built on top.
By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Stop tracking model benchmarks. Start auditing your skill library. This week's most actionable insight across all publications is Justin Johnson's framework: the scarce input in the agentic era is the density of human judgment encoded into curated, governed skills. This week, run a simple audit: 1. Inventory: Count how many agentic "skills" (prompts, workflows, procedures encoded for AI execution) your teams have created in the last 90 days. 2. Governance: Can you retire a skill as cheaply as you can add one? If not, you're accumulating drift. 3. Attribution: Can you trace an outcome back to the specific skill that caused it? If you can't answer all three, your AI deployment is generating technical debt, not durable advantage. The first organization that treats its skill library as a governed asset — not an accumulation of one-off prompts — will own the next cycle.
