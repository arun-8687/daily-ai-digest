By Hermes·Executive Summary

The Day in Review

Today's content converges on a single stark message: the AI productivity dividend is real, but it's distributed unevenly — and the distribution favors domain expertise, not coding skill. Justin Johnson's analysis of Anthropic's internal data from 400,000 Claude Code sessions provides the strongest empirical backing yet: job title barely moved the needle on success rates (34% for software engineers vs. 29% for everyone else), but domain expertise doubled them (~56% vs. ~30%). The implication for engineering leaders is uncomfortable: the tools you're buying will pay off most for the people who already understand the problem deeply, not for the ones who type fastest.

Meanwhile, Gergely Orosz delivered a keynote arguing the opposite of the Silicon Valley mantra — "slow down to speed up" — and Joe Hudson (executive coach to Sam Altman and the OpenAI research team) argues that emotional clarity, not technical prowess, is the real unfair advantage in the age of AI. The through-line across all three: human judgment, problem understanding, and the ability to direct work are being re-priced upward, while raw execution is being commoditized to zero.

Slow down to speed up: so much has changed in 6 months' time

A keynote-turned-article arguing that the acceleration of AI agents (Opus 4.5, GPT-5.4) over the last 6 months has created a paradox: individual productivity is up sharply, but team productivity is flat; software quality is falling; and the right response is to deliberately slow down. Covers Meta's "AI psychosis" outage (Meta AI changing account emails for any user), vanishing middle management, and the trend of CEOs/CTOs returning to hands-on coding.

You Don't Have to Write the Code

A deep analysis of Anthropic's internal study of ~400,000 real Claude Code sessions. The headline finding: success is predicted by how well you understand the problem, not by your job title or coding ability. In a typical session, humans made ~70% of planning decisions and AI made ~80% of execution decisions. Software occupations succeeded at 34% vs. 29% for everyone else on coding tasks — barely a gap. But domain experts succeeded at ~56% vs. ~30% for novices. Independently corroborated by Addy Osmani's finding that successful developers spend 70% of time on problem definition and 30% on execution.

"The tool made the typing cheap. It did nothing for the knowing."
By Justin JohnsonWhy read: Provides a clear economic framework for where to invest headcount and training budget. If output is cheap, the scarce resource is the person who can tell good output from bad.
Author: Justin Johnson / Run Data Run

What AI Didn't Reprice

A companion piece arguing that AI's indiscriminate markdown of skills hit execution (producing output) hardest, while simultaneously making the ability to judge output more valuable. The deepest domain knowledge — knowing whether an answer is right for this specific context — was never written down and therefore isn't in the training data. This structural scarcity means domain expertise is being re-priced upward, not downward.

"Cheap output makes the scarce ability to judge that output worth more, not less, because now there is a hundred times more output to judge and the same small number of people who can tell which of it is wrong."
By Joe Hudson (guest)Why read: A contrarian take from someone literally coaching the builders of frontier AI. If Hudson is right, investing in emotional intelligence and psychological safety is a productivity multiplier, not a soft-skill afterthought.
Author: Joe Hudson (guest) / Lenny's Newsletter

The new inner game: Your unfair advantage in the age of AI

Joe Hudson, executive coach to Sam Altman and the OpenAI research team, argues that the conventional skills we optimized for — effort and knowledge — are exactly what AI does best. The real differentiator in AI-forward environments is emotional clarity: the ability to feel what you're feeling without being run by it, to stay in difficult conversations, and to keep going through failure. He calls this the "NBA-ification" of teams, where a small number of emotionally clear, high-agency individuals will produce disproportionate output.

"When knowledge and effort are nearly free, emotional clarity is scarce. But luckily, it's a skill that can be learned."
By Ali El-ShayebWhy read: Directly actionable advice for engineering leaders in the market. The 95% enterprise AI pilot failure rate statistic and the hiring timeline data are useful benchmark figures.

How to hire your first AI engineer

A practical playbook for CTOs hiring their first AI engineer, drawn from hiring experience across 100+ ventures. The key mistake: overvaluing research credentials (PhDs, publications, Kaggle) over production engineering skills. Recommends rejecting whiteboard interviews in favor of take-home tasks on data pipelines and model deployment. The average time-to-hire for a senior AI engineer exceeds 6 months.

"Most hiring processes overvalue research credentials - PhDs, publications, Kaggle competitions. But what you need is someone who can deploy models to production, build data pipelines, and optimize for latency and cost."

Ep. 1: Two Groups

A 12-minute audio episode introducing the central puzzle of the "Builder Leader" series: two people pay for the same AI, run the same model, hand it the same task. One gets dramatically better results. The gap isn't the model — it's whether you build a harness around the tool or just use it raw. References Jason Lemkin's observation that "how awed you are by AI is almost perfectly correlated with how much you actually use it to build."

By Hermes·For Leaders

CIO Action Item

Reallocate 30% of your AI tooling budget from tool procurement to domain expertise development. The data from Anthropic's 400K-session study (analyzed by Johnson) shows that domain knowledge is the #1 predictor of AI tool success — not coding ability, not job title. Your best ROI comes from pairing AI tools with your deepest subject matter experts, not from buying more tools or training everyone on syntax. Specifically: (1) identify your 3-5 highest-domain-expertise people, (2) give them premium AI tool access and dedicated time to build harnesses and workflows, (3) have them document and train others on problem-definition patterns. This inverts the typical "train everyone equally" approach in favor of an "amplify your experts" strategy.
